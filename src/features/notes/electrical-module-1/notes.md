# Capacitor

Capacitance is the ability of a circuit element to store electric charge. Two conductors separated by an insulating material (the dielectric) form a capacitor.

If $Q$ is the stored charge in coulombs and $V$ is the applied voltage,

$$Q=CV,\qquad C=\frac{Q}{V}.$$

The unit of capacitance is the farad (F).

For a capacitor charged from zero to $V$, the average charging voltage is $V/2$. The stored energy is

$$E=\frac{1}{2}QV=\frac{1}{2}CV^2.$$

## 1.6 Resistances in series Based on ohms law

The circuit in which resistances are connected end to end so that there is only one path for current flow, is called a series circuit. In a series circuit,

1. the same current flows through all resistances,
2. there will be voltage drop across each resistance given by Ohm's law and
3. the sum of the voltage drops is equal to the applied voltage.

# VOLTAGE DIVIDER RULE

Rule: The **voltage across any resistor** in a series network is equal to the **ratio** of **its individual resistance** **to the** **total resistance of the series network**, multiplied by the total voltage in the circuit..



![Circuit diagram for voltage divider rule showing a voltage source V and two series resistors R1 and R2 with voltage drops V1 and V2](/notes/cse/s1/electrical/module-1/assets/page-039-image-1-source.png)



Figure 2 - Voltage Division Rule

$v_1$ = voltage across first resistor

$V$ = total voltage in the circuit

$R_1 + R_2$ = total resistance

$$
V_1 = \frac{VR_1}{R_1 + R_2}
$$

$$
V_2 = \frac{VR_2}{R_1 + R_2}
$$

---

---

<!-- page 40 | Module 1 · source page 40 -->

General Formula (N Resistors)

For a series connection of $N$ resistors, the voltage across any target resistor $R_x$ is:

$$V_x = V_{\text{total}} \cdot \left(\frac{R_x}{R_{\text{total}}}\right)$$

where $R_{\text{total}} = R_1 + R_2 + \dots + R_N$.

---

---

<!-- page 41 | Module 1 · source page 41 -->

According to Ohm's law,

$$V_1 = IR_1 \text{ and } V_2 = IR_2$$

Let $R$ is the total resistance of the circuit, and it is given by,

$$R = R_1 + R_2 \quad \cdots (1)$$

Also, from the circuit, we have,

$$V = IR = I(R_1 + R_2) \quad \cdots (2)$$

But

$$I = \frac{V_1}{R_1} = \frac{V_2}{R_2} \quad \cdots (3)$$

Therefore, from equations (2) & (3), we finally get,

$$V = \frac{V_1}{R_1}(R_1 + R_2) \implies V \cdot R_1 = V_1 \cdot (R_1 + R_2) \implies \frac{V \cdot R_1}{R_1 + R_2} = V_1$$

$$\therefore V_1 = \frac{VR_1}{R_1 + R_2} \quad \cdots (4)$$

Similarly,

$$V = \frac{V_2}{R_2}(R_1 + R_2)$$

$$\therefore V_2 = \frac{VR_2}{R_1 + R_2} \quad \cdots (5)$$

![Circuit vs Network comparison diagram showing a simple circuit with a battery, resistor, and capacitor, alongside a complex network of resistors.](/notes/cse/s1/electrical/module-1/assets/page-058-image-1-source.png)



* **Electrical circuit:** Electric circuit is an electrical network with at least one closed path.
* All circuits are networks; but, all networks are not circuits.
* **Loop:** A loop is any closed part of the circuit.
* **Mesh:** A mesh is the most elementary form of a loop and cannot be further divided into other loops.
* All meshes are loops but all loops are not meshes

- **To solve** this, we "remove" the shared current source branch and create a larger loop

# Matrix form RULE

- Diagonal Elements ($R_{11}, R_{22}, R_{33}$): Always Positive ($+$)
  - Sum of all resistances in that specific loop.
- Off-Diagonal Elements ($R_{12}, R_{23}, R_{31}$, etc.): Usually Negative ($-$)
  - Sum of resistances shared between two loops.
  - _Sign Rule_: If both loop currents pass through the shared resistor in **opposite directions**, the entry is **negative** ($-$). If they flow in the **same direction**, the entry is **positive** ($+$). Since all mesh currents are drawn clockwise by default, shared branches always have opposing currents, making off-diagonal terms negative.

If two voltage source is same loop For summing voltage element same polarity must be minus the voltage Opposite polarity add the voltage

For voltage matrix voltage polarity is depends on current flow direction

# Nodal voltage Matrix representation

In general, for any circuit, the node voltage equation can be written as
$$V/R=I$$
$$G \times V = I_S$$

where

$$G = \begin{bmatrix} G_{11} & -G_{12} & \dots & -G_{1n} \\ -G_{21} & G_{22} & \dots & -G_{nn} \\ \dots & \dots & \dots & \dots \\ G_{n1} & -G_{n2} & \dots & G_{nn} \end{bmatrix}$$

Matrix $G$ is called the conductance matrix.

conductance, $G = \frac{1}{R}$,

$$V = \begin{bmatrix} V_1 \\ V_2 \\ \dots \\ V_n \end{bmatrix}$$

$$I_S = \begin{bmatrix} I_{S1} \\ I_{S2} \\ \dots \\ I_{Sn} \end{bmatrix}$$

---

---

<!-- page 120 | Module 1 · source page 20 -->

# Compare mesh current matrix node voltage matrix

| Feature | Mesh Current Matrix Method $[R][I] = [V]$ | Node Voltage Matrix Method $[G][V] = [I]$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Governing Law | Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL) around loops | Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL) at nodes |
| Unknown Vector $(X)$ | Mesh currents $[I_1, I_2, \bm{\text{...}}, I_m]^T$ | Non-reference node voltages $[V_1, V_2, \bm{\text{...}}, V_n]^T$ |
| Coefficient Matrix $(A)$ | Resistance Matrix $[R]$ (Units: Ohms, $\Omega$) | Conductance Matrix $[G]$ (Units: Siemens, $S$) |
| Diagonal Elements $(A_{ii})$ | Sum of resistances in Mesh $i$ (Always positive) | Sum of conductances connected to Node $i$ (Always positive) |
| Off-Diagonal Elements ($A_{ij}$) | Negative sum of resistances shared between Mesh $i$ and Mesh $j$ | Negative sum of conductances connected directly between Node $i$ and Node $j$ |
| Source Vector $(B)$ | Net driving voltage in each mesh $[V]$ | Net injected current into each node $[I]$ |

---

Matrix Formats

1. Mesh Current Matrix Equation

$$\begin{bmatrix}
R_{11} & R_{12} & \\tau & R_{1m} \
R_{21} & R_{22} & \\tau & R_{2m} \\\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \
R_{m1} & R_{m2} & \\tau & R_{mm}
\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}
I_1 \
I_2 \
\vdots \
I_m
\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}
V_{s1} \
V_{s2} \
\vdots \
V_{sm}
\end{bmatrix}$$

* $R_{ii}$: Sum of all branch resistances bounding mesh $i$.
* $R_{ij}$ ($i \neq j$): $-(\text{resistance shared by mesh } i \text{ and mesh } j)$ when both mesh currents are defined clockwise.
* $V_{si}$: Sum of independent voltage source rises in the direction of mesh current $i$.

2. Node Voltage Matrix Equation

$$\begin{bmatrix}
G_{11} & G_{12} & \dots & G_{1n} \
G_{21} & G_{22} & \dots & G_{2n} \
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \
G_{n1} & G_{n2} & \dots & G_{nn}
\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}
V_1 \
V_2 \
\vdots \
V_n
\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}
I_{s1} \
I_{s2} \
\vdots \
I_{sn}
\end{bmatrix}$$

* $G_{ii}$: Sum of conductances ($1/R$) connected to node $i$.
* $G_{ij}$ ($i \neq j$): $-(\text{conductance connected directly between node } i \text{ and node } j)$.
* $I_{si}$: Net sum of independent source currents entering node $i$.

## STAR -- DELTA TRANSFORMATION



![Star and Delta resistive network transformation diagram and formulas](/notes/cse/s1/electrical/module-1/assets/page-044-image-1-source.png)



### DELTA to STAR TRANSFORMATION

$$R_A = \frac{R_{AB} R_{CA}}{R_{AB} + R_{BC} + R_{CA}}$$

$$R_B = \frac{R_{BC} R_{AB}}{R_{AB} + R_{BC} + R_{CA}}$$

$$R_C = \frac{R_{CA} R_{BC}}{R_{AB} + R_{BC} + R_{CA}}$$

### STAR to DELTA TRANSFORMATION

$$R_{AB} = \frac{R_A R_B + R_B R_C + R_C R_A}{R_C}$$

$$R_{BC} = \frac{R_A R_B + R_B R_C + R_C R_A}{R_A}$$

$$R_{CA} = \frac{R_A R_B + R_B R_C + R_C R_A}{R_B}$$

A material which possesses magnetic properties is called a magnet. A magnet attracts pieces of iron. When suspended freely, it points in the direction of North and South. ## 3.4 Magnetic flux

The total number of magnetic lines of force in a magnetic field is called the magnetic flux. It is denoted by $\Phi$ and its unit is Weber.

The properties of magnetic flux are listed below.

1. The magnetic flux lines are imaginary lines.
2. Each line of magnetic flux forms a closed loop.
3. Magnetic flux lines do not intersect each other.
4. They behave like stretched elastic threads and always try to shorten themselves.
5. The flux lines which are parallel and in same direction repel each other.

The magnetic flux lines always form closed paths. *The closed path followed by the flux lines is called a magnetic circuit.*

---

---

<!-- page 173 | Module 1 · source page 73 -->

## 3.5 Magnetic flux density

Magnetic flux density is the flux per unit area at right angles to the direction of the flux. If $\Phi$ is the flux and $A$ is the area of cross-section, then magnetic flux density denoted by $B$ is

$$B = \frac{\Phi}{A}$$

The unit of magnetic flux density is $\text{Wb/m}^2$ or Tesla.

## 3.6 Magneto motive force

Magneto Motive Force (MMF) is the force which establishes the magnetic flux in a magnetic circuit. When a current is passed through a coil, a magnetic flux is set up around it. The product of current ($I$) and number of turns ($N$) of the coil gives mmf.

MMF, $F$ is given by the expression,

$$F = NI$$

Its unit is Ampere-Turns (AT).

<!-- page 174 | Module 1 · source page 74 -->

## 3.7 Magnetic field intensity

Magnetic field intensity is the magneto motive force per unit length of the magnetic circuit. It is denoted by $H$ and its unit is Ampere-Turns/meter. Magnetic field intensity is also called *magnetic field strength* or *magnetizing force*.

$$H = \frac{\text{mmf}}{l} = \frac{F}{l}$$

$$H = \frac{NI}{l}$$

Or, $F = NI = Hl$ \dots(3.1)

## 3.8 Permeability

The ability of a material to conduct magnetic flux through it is called permeability of that material. It is represented by the letter $\mu$ and the unit is Henry/meter. The flux and hence the flux density $B$ are directly proportional to the magnetizing force $H$.

i.e.,

$$B \propto H$$

$$B = \mu H$$ (3.2)

$$\mu = \frac{B}{H}$$ (3.3)

where $\mu$ is the constant of proportionality and is called permeability of the magnetic material. Permeability of free space is denoted by $\mu_o$ and $\mu_o = 4\pi \times 10^{-7}$ Henry/meter.

---

---

<!-- page 175 | Module 1 · source page 75 -->

## 3.9 Relative permeability

When the mmf is applied to ferromagnetic materials, the flux produced is very large when compared with that in air, vacuum or nonmagnetic material. The ratio of the flux density produced in a material to the flux density produced in vacuum by the same magnetizing force is called relative permeability of that material. It is denoted by $\mu_r$.

$$\text{i.e., } \mu_r = \frac{\text{Flux density produced in a material}}{\text{Flux density in vacuum}}$$

Also,

$$\mu_r = \frac{\mu}{\mu_0}$$

The expression 3.2 can be rewritten as, $B = \mu_0 \mu_r H$

## 3.10 Reluctance

Reluctance of a material is its property of opposing the creation of magnetic flux in it. Reluctance is denoted by $S$ and is directly proportional to the length and inversely proportional to the area of cross-section of the magnetic path. It is analogous to resistance in an electrical circuit.

i.e.,

$$S \propto \frac{l}{A}$$

$$\boxed{S = \frac{l}{\mu_0 \mu_r A}} \tag{3.4}$$

where

$l = \text{mean length of magnetic circuit in meter}$
$A = \text{area of cross-section of magnetic circuit in } m^2$
$\mu_0 = \text{permeability of free space} = 4\pi \times 10^{-7} \text{ Henry/meter}$
$\mu_r = \text{relative permeability of magnetic material}$

Thus the reluctance is dependent on physical dimensions and the material of the magnetic circuit.

Also,

$$\text{Reluctance} = \frac{\text{mmf}}{\text{Flux}}$$

i.e., $$S = \frac{F}{\Phi}$$

From the expressions 3.1 and 3.2,

$$F = Hl = \frac{B}{\mu_0 \mu_r} l = \frac{\Phi}{A \mu_0 \mu_r} l \quad \text{since } B = \frac{\Phi}{A}$$

From equation 3.4, $F = S\Phi$

i.e., $\text{mmf} = \text{Reluctance} \times \text{Flux} \text{ (3.5)}$

$$\boxed{\text{Reluctance, } S = \frac{F}{\Phi} = \frac{NI}{\Phi} = \frac{Hl}{\Phi}}$$

The unit of reluctance is Ampere-Turn per Weber (AT/Wb).

Total mmf in the circuit,

Magnetic field intensity required for air, $H_g = \dfrac{B}{\mu_0}$

Therefore,

$$NI = H_i l_i + H_g l_g$$

# why this air gap?

An air gap ($l_g$) is intentional in magnetic cores (like inductors, flyback transformers, and DC-biased systems) to control magnetic flux and prevent core saturation.

- **Prevents Core Saturation:** High magnetic permeability in iron cores means they saturate quickly under high current or DC bias. The air gap acts as high magnetic reluctance, flattening the B-H curve so the core can handle higher currents without saturating.

- **Stores Magnetic Energy:** Ferromagnetic core materials hold very little energy, whereas air stores significant magnetic energy. The air gap serves as the primary energy storage region in devices like inductors and flyback transformers ($W = \frac{1}{2} \text{ }\text{·}\text{ } \frac{B^2}{\mu_0} \text{ }\text{·}\text{ } V_{\text{gap}}$).

- **Stabilizes Inductance:** An air gap makes total circuit reluctance dominated by the gap length rather than fluctuations in core permeability ($\mu_r$), keeping inductance ($L$) stable across temperature changes and varying operating currents.

- **Linearizes the Magnetic Circuit:** Air has a completely linear B-H response, reducing harmonic distortion in signal-processing inductors and audio transformers.
