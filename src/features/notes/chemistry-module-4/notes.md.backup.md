### Water characteristics and industrial needs

Water is an effective solvent for many ionic and polar substances used across domestic households, agriculture, and industry. In industrial plants, large volumes of water serve as process solvents, reaction media, and heat-transfer fluids for cooling and steam generation. Because natural water dissolves minerals from soil and rock, its suitability depends on the dissolved ionic species present.

**Hardness** is the characteristic of water that prevents rapid lathering with soap. It is caused by dissolved multivalent metallic cations—principally calcium ($\text{Ca}^{2+}$) and magnesium ($\text{Mg}^{2+}$), along with smaller amounts of other divalent or heavy metal ions such as $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ and $\text{Mn}^{2+}$.

* **Hard water** contains appreciable concentrations of dissolved $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ and $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ salts ($\text{Ca(HCO}_3)_2$, $\text{Mg(HCO}_3)_2$, $\text{CaCl}_2$, $\text{MgCl}_2$, $\text{CaSO}_4$, and $\text{MgSO}_4$). It does not lather readily with soap, instead forming a sticky white precipitate.
* **Soft water** contains negligible concentrations of $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ and $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ ions and produces a rich lather immediately upon agitation with soap.

### Chemical mechanism of soap scum formation

Commercial soaps are sodium or potassium salts of long-chain fatty acids, such as sodium stearate ($\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{35}\text{COONa}$). In soft water, sodium stearate dissolves freely to lower surface tension and form cleansing micelles. When soap is added to hard water, the dissolved $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ and $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ ions displace $\text{Na}^+$ to form insoluble calcium stearate or magnesium stearate precipitates, known as **soap scum**:

$$
2\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{35}\text{COONa (aq)} + \text{CaCl}_2\text{ (aq)} \longrightarrow (\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{35}\text{COO})_2\text{Ca}\downarrow\text{ (s)} + 2\text{NaCl (aq)}
$$

$$
2\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{35}\text{COONa (aq)} + \text{MgSO}_4\text{ (aq)} \longrightarrow (\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{35}\text{COO})_2\text{Mg}\downarrow\text{ (s)} + \text{Na}_2\text{SO}_4\text{ (aq)}
$$

Here, $(\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{35}\text{COO})_2\text{Ca}$ and $(\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{35}\text{COO})_2\text{Mg}$ precipitate as sticky grey-white curds that cling to fabrics and washbasins. Hardness ions consume soap before a persistent lather develops, increasing soap consumption.

### Temporary (carbonate) vs permanent (non-carbonate) hardness

Total hardness is divided into two categories based on the counter-anions present and their behaviour upon heating.

1. **Temporary hardness (carbonate hardness):** Caused by dissolved bicarbonates of calcium and magnesium, $\text{Ca(HCO}_3)_2$ and $\text{Mg(HCO}_3)_2$. It is called carbonate hardness because it reflects the portion of hardness chemically equivalent to the bicarbonate and carbonate alkalinity in the water. Boiling drives off dissolved carbon dioxide gas and shifts the carbonate equilibrium, precipitating insoluble calcium carbonate and, upon complete thermal decomposition, magnesium hydroxide (which has much lower solubility than magnesium carbonate):

$$
\text{Ca(HCO}_3)_2\text{ (aq)} \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{CaCO}_3\downarrow\text{ (s)} + \text{H}_2\text{O (l)} + \text{CO}_2\uparrow\text{ (g)}
$$

$$
\text{Mg(HCO}_3)_2\text{ (aq)} \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{Mg(OH)}_2\downarrow\text{ (s)} + 2\text{CO}_2\uparrow\text{ (g)}
$$

2. **Permanent hardness (non-carbonate hardness):** Caused by dissolved chlorides and sulfates of calcium and magnesium ($\text{CaCl}_2$, $\text{MgCl}_2$, $\text{CaSO}_4$, and $\text{MgSO}_4$). Because these salts do not decompose into insoluble precipitates when boiled, permanent hardness must be removed using chemical softening (such as the lime-soda process), ion-exchange resins, or membrane desalination.

| Feature | Temporary (Carbonate) Hardness | Permanent (Non-Carbonate) Hardness |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Causative salts** | $\text{Ca(HCO}_3)_2$, $\text{Mg(HCO}_3)_2$ | $\text{CaCl}_2$, $\text{MgCl}_2$, $\text{CaSO}_4$, $\text{MgSO}_4$ |
| **Effect of boiling** | Precipitates as $\text{CaCO}_3\downarrow$ and $\text{Mg(OH)}_2\downarrow$ | Not removed reliably by simple boiling |
| **Removal methods** | Simple boiling or calculated lime addition | Ion-exchange, reverse osmosis, or lime-soda process |

### Operational impacts of hard water

Using untreated hard water creates specific technical challenges across domestic and industrial operations:
* **Domestic laundering and bathing:** Soap is wasted precipitating $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ and $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ before any cleaning action begins, leaving sticky scum deposits on skin, fabrics, and bathroom fittings.
* **Industrial dyeing and sugar processing:** In textile dyeing, divalent cations react with dyes to produce uneven spots and off-shades. In sugar refining, calcium and magnesium salts impede uniform sucrose crystallization.
* **Steam boilers and heat exchangers:** When hard water is heated in boilers, bicarbonates and sulfates deposit as hard, adherent mineral **scales** ($\text{CaCO}_3$, $\text{CaSO}_4$) or loose **sludges** on heating surfaces. Because mineral scale has very low thermal conductivity, it acts as a thermal insulator, reducing heat transfer efficiency, wasting fuel, and causing dangerous localized overheating of boiler tubes. Furthermore, dissolved $\text{MgCl}_2$ undergoes high-temperature hydrolysis to generate corrosive hydrochloric acid ($\text{HCl}$), leading to severe boiler metal corrosion.

> **Watch out.** Hard water is not inherently toxic or unsafe to drink, nor does it universally prohibit concrete mixing; in fact, moderate calcium and magnesium levels contribute to dietary mineral intake. Conversely, "soft water" does not mean pure or microbiologically safe water—sodium-cycle softened water still contains dissolved salts, and any microbial contamination needs appropriate disinfection.

> **Watch out.** Do not confuse **hardness** (concentration of multivalent cations like $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ and $\text{Mg}^{2+}$) with **alkalinity** (acid-neutralizing capacity due to $\text{HCO}_3^-$, $\text{CO}_3^{2-}$, and $\text{OH}^-$) or **salinity** (total dissolved salts). Sodium salts such as $\text{NaCl}$ (salinity) and $\text{NaHCO}_3$ (alkalinity) do not contain divalent cations and therefore do not produce hardness.

### Self-check questions

1. **Question:** Why does a water sample containing $500\text{ mg/L}$ of dissolved $\text{NaCl}$ lather easily with dilute soap solution, whereas a sample containing $500\text{ mg/L}$ of $\text{CaCl}_2$ forms a sticky precipitate?
   **Answer:** Sodium chloride ($\text{NaCl}$) shares the $\text{Na}^+$ cation with sodium stearate soap and does not precipitate fatty acid anions, whereas $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ from $\text{CaCl}_2$ reacts with stearate anions to precipitate insoluble calcium stearate scum.
2. **Question:** Which solid precipitates form when water containing both $\text{Ca(HCO}_3)_2$ and $\text{Mg(HCO}_3)_2$ is boiled thoroughly?
   **Answer:** Insoluble calcium carbonate ($\text{CaCO}_3\downarrow$) and magnesium hydroxide ($\text{Mg(OH)}_2\downarrow$).

## Expressing hardness as calcium carbonate

### Why Calcium Carbonate is the Universal Reference

Natural water contains a mixture of dissolved calcium and magnesium salts—such as bicarbonates, sulfates, and chlorides—each having a different molar mass. Directly summing their mass concentrations in $\text{mg/L}$ is chemically meaningless because $1\text{ mg}$ of $\text{MgCl}_2$ contains a different number of divalent cations than $1\text{ mg}$ of $\text{CaSO}_4$. To compare and add these contributions on a uniform stoichiometric basis, water analysis expresses all hardness and alkalinity concentrations in terms of **equivalents of calcium carbonate ($\text{CaCO}_3$)**.

Calcium carbonate is chosen as this universal reference standard for three practical reasons:
1. **Convenient arithmetic:** Its molecular mass is exactly $100\text{ g/mol}$ (using standard rounded atomic masses) and its equivalent mass is $50\text{ g/eq}$, simplifying conversion calculations.
2. **Relevant chemistry:** $\text{CaCO}_3$ is a sparingly soluble scale-forming compound familiar from water softening.
3. **Primary analytical standard:** Pure, dry $\text{CaCO}_3$ can be dissolved in dilute $\text{HCl}$ to prepare accurate standard calcium solutions for calibrating water-testing titrations.

### Converting Concentrations to $\text{CaCO}_3$ Equivalents

Because chemical reactions occur equivalent-for-equivalent, the number of equivalents of a hardness-producing species equals the number of equivalents of $\text{CaCO}_3$ it represents:

$$
\frac{\text{Mass of species X}}{\text{Equivalent mass of X}} = \frac{\text{Mass of CaCO}_3\text{ equivalent}}{\text{Equivalent mass of CaCO}_3}
$$

For a species $X$ present at mass concentration $C_X$ (in $\text{mg/L}$) with equivalent mass $E_X$ (in $\text{g/eq}$ or $\text{mg/meq}$), the hardness $H$ expressed in $\text{mg/L as CaCO}_3$ is:

$$
H\text{ (mg/L as CaCO}_3\text{)} = C_X \times \frac{50}{E_X}
$$

The equivalent mass of an ion or salt is its formula mass $M$ divided by the total positive (or negative) charge $z$ exchanged per formula unit ($E_X = M/z$). For a salt containing a single divalent $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ or $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ ion ($z = 2$), $E_X = M/2$, which means:

$$
H\text{ (mg/L as CaCO}_3\text{)} = C_X \times \frac{50}{M/2} = C_X \times \frac{100}{M}
$$

> **Watch out.** The shortcut $H = C_X \times 100/M$ applies **only** when one formula unit supplies one divalent hardness cation (such as $\text{CaSO}_4$, $\text{MgCl}_2$, $\text{Ca}^{2+}$, or $\text{Mg}^{2+}$). For monovalent species like $\text{HCO}_3^-$ ($z=1$, $E = 61\text{ g/eq}$) or multivalent salts like $\text{Al}_2(\text{SO}_4)_3$ ($z = 2 \times 3 = 6$, $E = M/6$), you must use the fundamental equivalent-mass formula $C_X \times 50/E_X$.

Using standard rounded atomic masses ($\text{Ca} = 40$, $\text{Mg} = 24$, $\text{C} = 12$, $\text{H} = 1$, $\text{O} = 16$, $\text{S} = 32$, $\text{Cl} = 35.5$, $\text{Na} = 23$), the masses and roles of common water constituents are summarized below:

| Species | Formula / Atomic Mass ($M$, $\text{g/mol}$) | Charge Factor ($z$, $\text{eq/mol}$) | Equivalent Mass ($E$, $\text{g/eq}$) | Role in Water Analysis |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: | :--- |
| $\text{CaCO}_3$ | $100$ | $2$ | $50$ | Reference standard |
| $\text{CaSO}_4$ | $136$ | $2$ | $68$ | Non-carbonate hardness |
| $\text{MgSO}_4$ | $120$ | $2$ | $60$ | Non-carbonate hardness |
| $\text{CaCl}_2$ | $111$ | $2$ | $55.5$ | Non-carbonate hardness |
| $\text{MgCl}_2$ | $95$ | $2$ | $47.5$ | Non-carbonate hardness |
| $\text{Ca(HCO}_3\text{)}_2$ | $162$ | $2$ | $81$ | Carbonate hardness & alkalinity |
| $\text{Mg(HCO}_3\text{)}_2$ | $146$ | $2$ | $73$ | Carbonate hardness & alkalinity |
| $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ | $40$ | $2$ | $20$ | Total hardness cation |
| $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ | $24$ | $2$ | $12$ | Total hardness cation |
| $\text{HCO}_3^-$ | $61$ | $1$ | $61$ | Bicarbonate alkalinity |
| $\text{NaHCO}_3$ | $84$ | $1$ | $84$ | Alkalinity only (not a hardness contributor) |

### Units of Hardness and Concentration Conversions

Hardness is reported in several interconvertible units:
- **Milligrams per litre ($\text{mg/L}$):** The mass in milligrams of $\text{CaCO}_3$ equivalent present in $1\text{ L}$ of water.
- **Parts per million ($\text{ppm}$):** Parts of $\text{CaCO}_3$ equivalent per $10^6$ parts of water by mass. Because dilute aqueous solutions at room temperature have a density of almost exactly $1\text{ kg/L}$ ($10^6\text{ mg/L}$), $1\text{ mg/L} \approx 1\text{ ppm}$.
- **Milliequivalents per litre ($\text{meq/L}$):** Since $1\text{ meq}$ of $\text{CaCO}_3$ has a mass of $50\text{ mg}$, $1\text{ meq/L} = 50\text{ mg/L as CaCO}_3$.
- **Degrees French ($^\circ\text{Fr}$):** Parts of $\text{CaCO}_3$ equivalent per $10^5$ parts of water; $1^\circ\text{Fr} = 10\text{ mg/L as CaCO}_3$.
- **Clarke's degree ($^\circ\text{Cl}$):** One grain of $\text{CaCO}_3$ equivalent per Imperial gallon; $1^\circ\text{Cl} = 14.25\text{ mg/L as CaCO}_3$.

When the concentration of a dissolved salt is given in molarity ($\text{mol/L}$) or normality ($\text{eq/L}$), convert it to $\text{mg/L as CaCO}_3$ using dimensional analysis:

$$
\text{Normality } N\text{ (eq/L)} = \text{Molarity } M\text{ (mol/L)} \times z\text{ (eq/mol)}
$$

$$
H\text{ (mg/L as CaCO}_3\text{)} = N\text{ (eq/L)} \times 50\text{ (g/eq)} \times 1000\text{ (mg/g)} = N \times 50,000
$$

> **Watch out.** Normality is a reaction-specific concentration unit in $\text{eq/L}$, never a mass ratio in $\text{ppm}$, and you must always include the $/\text{L}$ in your final hardness unit ($\text{mg/L}$, not $\text{mg}$).

See [Worked problems on water hardness](#page-3) for salt, ion, molarity and normality calculations.

### Total, Carbonate, and Non-Carbonate Hardness

When water analysis reports individual ion concentrations rather than hypothetical salt combinations, hardness is partitioned by comparing **Total Hardness ($\text{TH}$)** with **Total Alkalinity ($\text{TA}$)**, both expressed in $\text{mg/L as CaCO}_3$:
1. **Total Hardness ($\text{TH}$):** The sum of $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ and $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ equivalents:

$$
\text{TH} = \left(C_{\text{Ca}^{2+}} \times \frac{50}{20}\right) + \left(C_{\text{Mg}^{2+}} \times \frac{50}{12}\right)
$$

2. **Bicarbonate Alkalinity:** For natural waters where bicarbonate ($\text{HCO}_3^-$) is the sole alkalinity species:

$$
\text{TA} = C_{\text{HCO}_3^-} \times \frac{50}{61}
$$

3. **Carbonate (Temporary) Hardness ($\text{CH}$):** The portion of total hardness chemically associated with carbonate and bicarbonate ions. Because carbonate hardness cannot exceed the total divalent cations actually present, $\text{CH}$ is the smaller of $\text{TH}$ and $\text{TA}$:

$$
\text{CH} = \min(\text{TH}, \text{TA})
$$

4. **Non-Carbonate (Permanent) Hardness ($\text{NCH}$):** The remaining hardness paired with sulfate, chloride, or nitrate anions:

$$
\text{NCH} = \text{TH} - \text{CH}
$$

If $\text{TA} \ge \text{TH}$ (which can occur when sufficient sodium bicarbonate, $\text{NaHCO}_3$, is present), then $\text{CH} = \text{TH}$ and $\text{NCH} = 0$. Adding $\text{NaHCO}_3$ to water containing $\text{CaSO}_4$ does **not** increase $\text{TH}$ (because $\text{Na}^+$ does not cause hardness), but it increases $\text{TA}$, shifting hardness classification from $\text{NCH}$ to $\text{CH}$. Conversely, neutral sodium salts like $\text{NaCl}$ contribute neither $\text{Ca}^{2+}/\text{Mg}^{2+}$ nor $\text{HCO}_3^-$, changing neither $\text{TH}$ nor $\text{TA}$. Note that classifying hardness as $\text{CH}$ is a stoichiometric accounting definition, not a guarantee of complete quantitative removal by simple boiling, since $\text{CaCO}_3$ and $\text{Mg(OH)}_2$ retain slight finite solubilities.

### Self-Check Questions

1. **Question:** A water sample has $\text{TH} = 150\text{ mg/L}$ and bicarbonate alkalinity $= 200\text{ mg/L}$ (both as $\text{CaCO}_3$). Find the carbonate and non-carbonate hardness.
   **Answer:** Since $\text{CH} = \min(\text{TH}, \text{TA})$, $\text{CH} = \min(150, 200) = 150\text{ mg/L as CaCO}_3$, and $\text{NCH} = 150 - 150 = 0\text{ mg/L as CaCO}_3$. The extra $50\text{ mg/L}$ of alkalinity is associated with non-hardness cations such as $\text{Na}^+$.

2. **Question:** Why is the equivalent mass of $\text{HCO}_3^-$ taken as $61\text{ g/eq}$ when calculating alkalinity in $\text{CaCO}_3$ equivalents, even though $1\text{ mol}$ of $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ reacts with $2\text{ mol}$ of $\text{HCO}_3^-$?
   **Answer:** Equivalent mass is defined per unit of ionic charge ($E = M/|z|$). Because $\text{HCO}_3^-$ carries a charge of $-1$, $1\text{ mol}$ ($61\text{ g}$) equals $1\text{ eq}$. Two equivalents of $\text{HCO}_3^-$ ($2 \times 61 = 122\text{ g}$) naturally balance $1\text{ mol}$ ($2\text{ eq}$) of $\text{Ca}^{2+}$.

## Worked problems on water hardness

### Summary of formulas and reference data

To compare waters containing different dissolved minerals, every hardness-producing species—and every alkalinity-producing species used to partition carbonate (temporary) and non-carbonate (permanent) hardness—is converted into its chemical equivalent of calcium carbonate ($\text{CaCO}_3$). Because the molar mass of $\text{CaCO}_3$ is $100\text{ g/mol}$ and its ionic charge factor $z = 2$, the equivalent mass of $\text{CaCO}_3$ is $E_{\text{CaCO}_3} = 50\text{ g/eq}$.

For any dissolved salt or ion $i$ with mass concentration $C_i$ (in $\text{mg/L}$ or $\text{ppm}$) and equivalent mass $E_i$ (in $\text{g/eq}$):

$$
\text{CaCO}_3\text{ equivalent} = \frac{C_i \times 50}{E_i}\text{ (in mg/L or ppm as CaCO}_3\text{)}
$$

When solution concentrations are reported in normality $N$ ($\text{eq/L}$) or molarity $M$ ($\text{mol/L}$), where $N = M \times z$ and $z$ is the total positive charge per formula unit:

$$
\text{Hardness} = N \times 50 \times 1000\text{ mg/L as CaCO}_3
$$

| Species | Formula | Atomic / Molar mass ($\text{g/mol}$) | Charge factor ($z$) | Equivalent mass ($E_i$, $\text{g/eq}$) |
| :--- | :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| Calcium carbonate | $\text{CaCO}_3$ | $40 + 12 + 48 = 100$ | $2$ | $50$ |
| Calcium bicarbonate | $\text{Ca(HCO}_3)_2$ | $40 + 2(1 + 12 + 48) = 162$ | $2$ | $81$ |
| Magnesium bicarbonate | $\text{Mg(HCO}_3)_2$ | $24 + 2(1 + 12 + 48) = 146$ | $2$ | $73$ |
| Calcium sulfate | $\text{CaSO}_4$ | $40 + 32 + 64 = 136$ | $2$ | $68$ |
| Magnesium sulfate | $\text{MgSO}_4$ | $24 + 32 + 64 = 120$ | $2$ | $60$ |
| Calcium chloride | $\text{CaCl}_2$ | $40 + 2(35.5) = 111$ | $2$ | $55.5$ |
| Magnesium chloride | $\text{MgCl}_2$ | $24 + 2(35.5) = 95$ | $2$ | $47.5$ |
| Sodium bicarbonate | $\text{NaHCO}_3$ | $23 + 1 + 12 + 48 = 84$ | $1$ | $84$ |
| Calcium ion | $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ | $40$ | $2$ | $20$ |
| Magnesium ion | $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ | $24$ | $2$ | $12$ |
| Bicarbonate ion | $\text{HCO}_3^-$ | $1 + 12 + 48 = 61$ | $1$ | $61$ |

### Rules for partitioning hardness

1. **Total Hardness ($\text{TH}$):** Sum the $\text{CaCO}_3$ equivalents of all multivalent hardness cations ($\text{Ca}^{2+}$ and $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ salts or ions). Alkali metal salts ($\text{Na}^+$, $\text{K}^+$) do **not** contribute to $\text{TH}$.
2. **Alkalinity ($\text{Alk}$):** Sum the $\text{CaCO}_3$ equivalents of all bicarbonate and carbonate species present, **including** sodium bicarbonate ($\text{NaHCO}_3$).
3. **Carbonate / Temporary Hardness ($\text{CH}$):** $\text{CH} = \min(\text{TH}, \text{Alk})$. If alkalinity is less than total hardness, $\text{CH} = \text{Alk}$. If alkalinity equals or exceeds total hardness, $\text{CH} = \text{TH}$.
4. **Non-Carbonate / Permanent Hardness ($\text{NCH}$):** $\text{NCH} = \text{TH} - \text{CH}$. Because $\text{CH}$ can never exceed $\text{TH}$, permanent hardness is never negative.

> **Watch out.** Do not round individual salt equivalents to two decimal places and then add them. Truncating intermediate values (for example, writing $2.46$ instead of $2.4691$ for $4\text{ ppm Ca(HCO}_3)_2$) introduces cumulative rounding errors in the final total. Keep at least four decimal places during intermediate steps.

### Worked examples

#### Example 1: Single permanent hardness salt ($\text{CaSO}_4$)

**Problem:** A water sample contains $408\text{ mg}$ of $\text{CaSO}_4$ per litre. Calculate its hardness in terms of $\text{CaCO}_3$ equivalence.

**Solution:**

From the reference table, the molar mass of $\text{CaSO}_4$ is $136\text{ g/mol}$ and its equivalent mass is $E_{\text{CaSO}_4} = \frac{136}{2} = 68\text{ g/eq}$. Given $C_{\text{CaSO}_4} = 408\text{ mg/L}$:

$$
\text{Hardness} = \frac{408 \times 50}{68} = 300\text{ mg/L as CaCO}_3\text{ (or } 300\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3\text{)}
$$

Because $\text{CaSO}_4$ is a sulfate salt, this entire $300\text{ mg/L as CaCO}_3$ is permanent (non-carbonate) hardness.

#### Example 2: Single temporary hardness salt ($\text{Ca(HCO}_3)_2$)

**Problem:** Calculate the hardness of a water sample containing $4\text{ ppm}$ of calcium bicarbonate, $\text{Ca(HCO}_3)_2$.

**Solution:**

The molar mass of $\text{Ca(HCO}_3)_2$ is $162\text{ g/mol}$ and its equivalent mass is $E_{\text{Ca(HCO}_3)_2} = \frac{162}{2} = 81\text{ g/eq}$. Converting $4\text{ ppm}$ into $\text{CaCO}_3$ equivalence:

$$
\text{Hardness} = \frac{4 \times 50}{81} = \frac{200}{81} = 2.4691\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3 \approx 2.47\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3
$$

#### Example 3: Mixed bicarbonate and sulfate salts

**Problem:** Calculate the temporary (carbonate), permanent (non-carbonate), and total hardness of a water sample having the following composition: $\text{Ca(HCO}_3)_2 = 4\text{ ppm}$, $\text{Mg(HCO}_3)_2 = 6\text{ ppm}$, $\text{CaSO}_4 = 8\text{ ppm}$, and $\text{MgSO}_4 = 10\text{ ppm}$.

**Solution:**

Convert each hardness-producing salt to its $\text{CaCO}_3$ equivalent using unrounded fractions first:

* $\text{Ca(HCO}_3)_2$ ($E = 81\text{ g/eq}$): $\frac{4 \times 50}{81} = \frac{200}{81} = 2.4691\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3$
* $\text{Mg(HCO}_3)_2$ ($E = 73\text{ g/eq}$): $\frac{6 \times 50}{73} = \frac{300}{73} = 4.1096\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3$
* $\text{CaSO}_4$ ($E = 68\text{ g/eq}$): $\frac{8 \times 50}{68} = \frac{400}{68} = 5.8824\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3$
* $\text{MgSO}_4$ ($E = 60\text{ g/eq}$): $\frac{10 \times 50}{60} = \frac{500}{60} = 8.3333\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3$

Now group into carbonate (temporary) and non-carbonate (permanent) contributions:

$$
\text{Temporary hardness (CH)} = 2.4691 + 4.1096 = 6.5787\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3 \approx 6.58\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3
$$

$$
\text{Permanent hardness (NCH)} = 5.8824 + 8.3333 = 14.2157\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3 \approx 14.22\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3
$$

$$
\text{Total hardness (TH)} = 6.5787 + 14.2157 = 20.7944\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3 \approx 20.79\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3
$$

#### Example 4: Mixed bicarbonate, sulfate, and chloride salts

**Problem:** Calculate the temporary, permanent, and total hardness of water containing $\text{Ca(HCO}_3)_2 = 8.1\text{ mg/L}$, $\text{Mg(HCO}_3)_2 = 14.6\text{ mg/L}$, $\text{CaSO}_4 = 13.6\text{ mg/L}$, and $\text{MgCl}_2 = 19\text{ mg/L}$.

**Solution:**

Calculate the $\text{CaCO}_3$ equivalent of each salt:

* $\text{Ca(HCO}_3)_2$ ($E = 81\text{ g/eq}$): $\frac{8.1 \times 50}{81} = 5.0\text{ mg/L as CaCO}_3$
* $\text{Mg(HCO}_3)_2$ ($E = 73\text{ g/eq}$): $\frac{14.6 \times 50}{73} = 10.0\text{ mg/L as CaCO}_3$
* $\text{CaSO}_4$ ($E = 68\text{ g/eq}$): $\frac{13.6 \times 50}{68} = 10.0\text{ mg/L as CaCO}_3$
* $\text{MgCl}_2$ ($E = 47.5\text{ g/eq}$): $\frac{19 \times 50}{47.5} = 20.0\text{ mg/L as CaCO}_3$

Sum the respective components:

$$
\text{Temporary hardness (CH)} = 5.0 + 10.0 = 15.0\text{ mg/L as CaCO}_3
$$

$$
\text{Permanent hardness (NCH)} = 10.0 + 20.0 = 30.0\text{ mg/L as CaCO}_3
$$

$$
\text{Total hardness (TH)} = 15.0 + 30.0 = 45.0\text{ mg/L as CaCO}_3
$$

#### Example 5: Effect of sodium bicarbonate ($\text{NaHCO}_3$) on hardness partitioning

**Problem:** Calculate the temporary, permanent, and total hardness of a water sample containing $\text{Ca(HCO}_3)_2 = 4\text{ ppm}$, $\text{Mg(HCO}_3)_2 = 6\text{ ppm}$, $\text{CaSO}_4 = 8\text{ ppm}$, $\text{MgSO}_4 = 10\text{ ppm}$, and $\text{NaHCO}_3 = 3\text{ ppm}$.

**Solution:**

Why does $\text{NaHCO}_3$ alter the distribution of hardness without changing total hardness? Sodium ions ($\text{Na}^+$) do not precipitate soap or form boiler scale, so $\text{NaHCO}_3$ adds zero total hardness. However, its bicarbonate ion ($\text{HCO}_3^-$) increases the total alkalinity of the water. Upon boiling, this extra $\text{HCO}_3^-$ reacts with $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ or $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ ions originally paired with $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$, precipitating them as carbonate scale. Thus, $\text{NaHCO}_3$ converts an equivalent amount of permanent hardness into temporary hardness while leaving $\text{TH}$ unchanged.

From Example 3, the four $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ and $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ salts give:

* $\text{Total hardness (TH)} = 2.4691 + 4.1096 + 5.8824 + 8.3333 = 20.7944\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3$
* Bicarbonate alkalinity from $\text{Ca(HCO}_3)_2$ and $\text{Mg(HCO}_3)_2 = 2.4691 + 4.1096 = 6.5787\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3$

Now find the alkalinity equivalence of $\text{NaHCO}_3$ ($E = 84\text{ g/eq}$):

$$
\text{Alkalinity from NaHCO}_3 = \frac{3 \times 50}{84} = \frac{150}{84} = 1.7857\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3
$$

$$
\text{Total alkalinity (Alk)} = 6.5787 + 1.7857 = 8.3644\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3
$$

Since $\text{Alk} < \text{TH}$, temporary (carbonate) hardness equals total alkalinity:

$$
\text{Temporary hardness (CH)} = 8.3644\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3 \approx 8.36\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3
$$

$$
\text{Permanent hardness (NCH)} = \text{TH} - \text{CH} = 20.7944 - 8.3644 = 12.4300\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3 \approx 12.43\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3
$$

$$
\text{Total hardness (TH)} = 20.7944\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3 \approx 20.79\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3
$$

#### Example 6: Second mixture containing sodium bicarbonate ($\text{NaHCO}_3$)

**Problem:** Calculate the temporary, permanent, and total hardness of water having the composition: $\text{Ca(HCO}_3)_2 = 10\text{ ppm}$, $\text{Mg(HCO}_3)_2 = 15\text{ ppm}$, $\text{CaSO}_4 = 8\text{ ppm}$, $\text{MgSO}_4 = 12\text{ ppm}$, and $\text{NaHCO}_3 = 3\text{ ppm}$.

**Solution:**

Convert the four divalent hardness salts to $\text{CaCO}_3$ equivalents:

* $\text{Ca(HCO}_3)_2$ ($E = 81\text{ g/eq}$): $\frac{10 \times 50}{81} = 6.1728\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3$
* $\text{Mg(HCO}_3)_2$ ($E = 73\text{ g/eq}$): $\frac{15 \times 50}{73} = 10.2740\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3$
* $\text{CaSO}_4$ ($E = 68\text{ g/eq}$): $\frac{8 \times 50}{68} = 5.8824\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3$
* $\text{MgSO}_4$ ($E = 60\text{ g/eq}$): $\frac{12 \times 50}{60} = 10.0000\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3$

$$
\text{Total hardness (TH)} = 6.1728 + 10.2740 + 5.8824 + 10.0000 = 32.3292\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3 \approx 32.33\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3
$$

Include the $\text{CaCO}_3$ equivalent of $\text{NaHCO}_3$ ($\frac{3 \times 50}{84} = 1.7857\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3$) in the alkalinity sum:

$$
\text{Temporary hardness (CH)} = 6.1728 + 10.2740 + 1.7857 = 18.2325\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3 \approx 18.23\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3
$$

$$
\text{Permanent hardness (NCH)} = \text{TH} - \text{CH} = 32.329165\ldots - 18.232526\ldots = 14.096639\ldots\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3 \approx 14.10\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3
$$

#### Example 7: Mixture containing neutral sodium chloride ($\text{NaCl}$)

**Problem:** Calculate the temporary, permanent, and total hardness of water containing $\text{Ca(HCO}_3)_2 = 10\text{ ppm}$, $\text{Mg(HCO}_3)_2 = 15\text{ ppm}$, $\text{CaCl}_2 = 8\text{ ppm}$, $\text{MgCl}_2 = 12\text{ ppm}$, and $\text{NaCl} = 3\text{ ppm}$.

**Solution:**

> **Watch out.** Unlike $\text{NaHCO}_3$, sodium chloride ($\text{NaCl}$) contains neither a multivalent hardness cation ($\text{Ca}^{2+}$, $\text{Mg}^{2+}$) nor a carbonate/bicarbonate alkalinity anion ($\text{HCO}_3^-$, $\text{CO}_3^{2-}$). Therefore, $\text{NaCl}$ has **no effect** on temporary, permanent, or total hardness. Never subtract or add an $\text{NaCl}$ equivalent to permanent or temporary hardness.

Calculate the $\text{CaCO}_3$ equivalents of the four $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ and $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ salts:

* $\text{Ca(HCO}_3)_2$ ($E = 81\text{ g/eq}$): $\frac{10 \times 50}{81} = 6.1728\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3$
* $\text{Mg(HCO}_3)_2$ ($E = 73\text{ g/eq}$): $\frac{15 \times 50}{73} = 10.2740\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3$
* $\text{CaCl}_2$ ($E = 55.5\text{ g/eq}$): $\frac{8 \times 50}{55.5} = 7.2072\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3$
* $\text{MgCl}_2$ ($E = 47.5\text{ g/eq}$): $\frac{12 \times 50}{47.5} = 12.6316\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3$
* $\text{NaCl} = 3\text{ ppm}$: Ignored ($0\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3$ contribution to both hardness and alkalinity).

Sum the carbonate and non-carbonate contributions:

$$
\text{Temporary hardness (CH)} = 6.1728 + 10.2740 = 16.4468\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3 \approx 16.45\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3
$$

$$
\text{Permanent hardness (NCH)} = 7.2072 + 12.6316 = 19.8388\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3 \approx 19.84\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3
$$

$$
\text{Total hardness (TH)} = 16.4468 + 19.8388 = 36.2856\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3 \approx 36.29\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3
$$

#### Example 8: Ion-basis water analysis where $\text{TH} > \text{Alk}$

**Problem:** Calculate the temporary, permanent, and total hardness of a water sample whose ionic analysis reports $\text{Ca}^{2+} = 200\text{ ppm}$, $\text{Mg}^{2+} = 96\text{ ppm}$, and $\text{HCO}_3^- = 976\text{ ppm}$ (these are the relevant entries of a partial ion analysis).

**Solution:**

When an analysis is given in terms of individual ions rather than combined salts, total hardness depends solely on the hardness cations ($\text{Ca}^{2+}$ and $\text{Mg}^{2+}$), while temporary hardness is governed by the bicarbonate alkalinity ($\text{HCO}_3^-$). Monovalent ions ($\text{Na}^+$, $\text{Cl}^-$) and unaligned sulfate figures from incomplete ion balances are not needed.

1. **Total Hardness ($\text{TH}$):**
   * $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ ($E = 20\text{ g/eq}$): $\frac{200 \times 50}{20} = 500\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3$
   * $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ ($E = 12\text{ g/eq}$): $\frac{96 \times 50}{12} = 400\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3$

$$
\text{TH} = 500 + 400 = 900\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3
$$

2. **Bicarbonate Alkalinity ($\text{Alk}$):**
   * $\text{HCO}_3^-$ ($E = 61\text{ g/eq}$): $\frac{976 \times 50}{61} = 800\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3$

3. **Carbonate and Non-Carbonate Hardness:**
   Because $\text{Alk} (800\text{ ppm}) < \text{TH} (900\text{ ppm})$:

$$
\text{Temporary hardness (CH)} = 800\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3
$$

$$
\text{Permanent hardness (NCH)} = \text{TH} - \text{CH} = 900 - 800 = 100\text{ ppm as CaCO}_3
$$

#### Example 9: Ion-basis water analysis where alkalinity exceeds total hardness ($\text{Alk} > \text{TH}$)

**Problem:** A sample of water on analysis gave the following results: $\text{Ca}^{2+} = 30\text{ mg/L}$, $\text{Mg}^{2+} = 18\text{ mg/L}$, $\text{HCO}_3^- = 244\text{ mg/L}$, and $\text{Na}^+ = 11.5\text{ mg/L}$. Calculate the temporary, permanent, and total hardness.

**Solution:**

1. **Total Hardness ($\text{TH}$):**
   * $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ ($E = 20\text{ g/eq}$): $\frac{30 \times 50}{20} = 75\text{ mg/L as CaCO}_3$
   * $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ ($E = 12\text{ g/eq}$): $\frac{18 \times 50}{12} = 75\text{ mg/L as CaCO}_3$

$$
\text{TH} = 75 + 75 = 150\text{ mg/L as CaCO}_3
$$

2. **Bicarbonate Alkalinity ($\text{Alk}$):**
   * $\text{HCO}_3^-$ ($E = 61\text{ g/eq}$): $\frac{244 \times 50}{61} = 200\text{ mg/L as CaCO}_3$

3. **Partitioning CH and NCH:**
   Here, the total bicarbonate alkalinity ($200\text{ mg/L as CaCO}_3$) exceeds the total hardness ($150\text{ mg/L as CaCO}_3$) ; the reported ion list is incomplete and must not be treated as a charge-balanced full analysis. Sodium does not contribute to total hardness. Because temporary (carbonate) hardness can **never** exceed total hardness ($\text{CH} = \min(\text{TH}, \text{Alk})$):

$$
\text{Temporary hardness (CH)} = \text{TH} = 150\text{ mg/L as CaCO}_3
$$

$$
\text{Permanent hardness (NCH)} = \text{TH} - \text{CH} = 150 - 150 = 0\text{ mg/L as CaCO}_3
$$

*(Do not report temporary hardness as $200\text{ mg/L}$ or permanent hardness as $-50\text{ mg/L}$; $200\text{ mg/L as CaCO}_3$ is the alkalinity, not the temporary hardness.)*

#### Example 10: Hardness from normality and molarity of $\text{MgSO}_4$ and $\text{CaCl}_2$ solutions

**Problem:** Calculate the hardness (in $\text{mg/L as CaCO}_3$) of:
(i) $0.08\text{ N MgSO}_4$ solution
(ii) $0.05\text{ M CaCl}_2$ solution

**Solution:**

**(i) $0.08\text{ N MgSO}_4$ solution:**
Using $\text{Hardness} = N \times E_{\text{CaCO}_3} \times 1000$:

$$
\text{Hardness} = 0.08\text{ eq/L} \times 50\text{ g/eq} \times 1000\text{ mg/g} = 4000\text{ mg/L as CaCO}_3
$$

**(ii) $0.05\text{ M CaCl}_2$ solution:**
Each formula unit of $\text{CaCl}_2$ releases one $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ ion ($z = 2$). First convert molarity to normality ($\text{eq/L}$):

$$
N = M \times z = 0.05\text{ mol/L} \times 2\text{ eq/mol} = 0.10\text{ N}
$$

$$
\text{Hardness} = 0.10\text{ eq/L} \times 50\text{ g/eq} \times 1000\text{ mg/g} = 5000\text{ mg/L as CaCO}_3
$$

#### Example 11: Formal $\text{CaCO}_3$ charge equivalence of $0.02\text{ M Al}_2(\text{SO}_4)_3$

**Problem:** Calculate the formal $\text{CaCO}_3$ equivalent concentration of a $0.02\text{ M Al}_2(\text{SO}_4)_3$ solution.

**Solution:**

One formula unit of $\text{Al}_2(\text{SO}_4)_3$ dissociates into two $\text{Al}^{3+}$ cations, giving a total positive charge factor $z = 2 \times 3 = 6\text{ eq/mol}$.

$$
N = M \times z = 0.02\text{ mol/L} \times 6\text{ eq/mol} = 0.12\text{ N}
$$

$$
\text{Formal CaCO}_3\text{ equivalent} = 0.12\text{ eq/L} \times 50\text{ g/eq} \times 1000\text{ mg/g} = 6000\text{ mg/L as CaCO}_3
$$

*Important chemical caveat:* In water quality practice, routine hardness is defined by $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ and $\text{Mg}^{2+}$. Aluminium salts such as $\text{Al}_2(\text{SO}_4)_3$ (alum) undergo rapid acid hydrolysis in water to form insoluble $\text{Al(OH)}_3$ flocs during coagulation rather than remaining as simple dissolved hardness ions. Therefore, this calculation represents a **formal stoichiometric charge equivalence** used in textbook exercises, not a standard natural-water hardness measurement.

By contrast, $\text{NaHCO}_3$ supplies $\text{HCO}_3^-$ alkalinity, which pairs with non-carbonate $\text{Ca}^{2+}/\text{Mg}^{2+}$ ions upon boiling, increasing the carbonate portion up to the total hardness; once all hardness is carbonate hardness, further bicarbonate cannot increase it.

## Ion-exchange softening and demineralization

Ion exchange is a reversible heterogeneous process in which mobile ions from an aqueous solution are exchanged for stoichiometrically equivalent, similarly charged ions electrostatically bound to an insoluble solid matrix. In water treatment, these solid matrices are synthetic organic polymers known as **ion-exchange resins**. They possess a high-molecular-weight, porous, three-dimensional cross-linked structure—most commonly a styrene–divinylbenzene copolymer—that prevents the resin from dissolving in water while allowing dissolved ions to diffuse freely through its micropores.

Each resin consists of two distinct ionic components:
* **Fixed functional groups:** Covalently bonded to the immobile polymer backbone, carrying a charge when ionized; strong-acid sulfonate and strong-base quaternary ammonium resins remain charged over broad working pH ranges.
* **Mobile counter-ions:** Oppositely charged ions loosely associated with the fixed groups that can be reversibly swapped with ions of the same charge sign from the surrounding water.

> **Watch out.** Student notes sometimes misspell the process as the "iron exchange process" or write resin formulas as $RH^+$ and $ROH^-$, which implies the entire resin carries a net charge. Always represent the neutral resin unit as a fixed polymer ion paired with a mobile counter-ion, such as $R^-H^+$ (or simply $RH$) for a cation exchanger and $R'^+OH^-$ (or $R'OH$) for an anion exchanger.

### Softening versus demineralization

It is important to distinguish between simple **water softening** and complete **demineralization (deionization)**:
* **Sodium-cycle cation softening:** Uses a cation-exchange resin in the sodium form ($RNa$). As hard water passes through the bed, hardness-causing divalent cations ($Ca^{2+}$ and $Mg^{2+}$) displace $Na^+$ ions on the resin:
  $$
  2RNa + Ca^{2+} \longrightarrow R_2Ca + 2Na^+
  $$
  Although the effluent is soft because calcium and magnesium are removed, it still contains dissolved sodium salts ($NaCl$, $Na_2SO_4$) and all the original anions. Thus, the water is *softened*, not demineralized.
* **Two-bed demineralization:** Uses a hydrogen-form cation-exchange resin ($RH$) followed in series by a hydroxide-form anion-exchange resin ($R'OH$). Because cations are replaced by $H^+$ and anions are replaced by $OH^-$, the two counter-ions combine to form pure water ($H_2O$), removing virtually all dissolved mineral salts.

### Classification of demineralization resins

| Resin Type | Polymer Matrix & Functional Group | Fixed Group | Mobile Counter-Ion | Target Ions Removed |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Cation Exchange Resin (CER)** | Sulphonated or carboxylated styrene–divinylbenzene copolymer | Sulfonate ($-SO_3^-$) or carboxylate ($-COO^-$) | $H^+$ | $Ca^{2+}, Mg^{2+}, Na^+, Fe^{2+}$ |
| **Anion Exchange Resin (AER)** | Quaternary ammonium styrene–divinylbenzene or amine–formaldehyde copolymer | Quaternary ammonium ($-NR_3^+$) | $OH^-$ | $Cl^-, SO_4^{2-}, HCO_3^-, NO_3^-$ |

#### 1. Cation exchange resins ($RH$)
Cation exchangers contain acidic functional groups, typically formed by sulphonating a cross-linked styrene–divinylbenzene copolymer so that sulfonate groups ($-SO_3^-H^+$) are attached to the aromatic rings. In the hydrogen form, they release $H^+$ ions in exchange for cations in hard water.

*Figure 4.1: Structure of a sulphonated styrene–divinylbenzene cation-exchange resin with fixed $-SO_3^-$ groups and mobile $H^+$ counter-ions.*

#### 2. Anion exchange resins ($R'OH$)
Anion exchangers are synthesized by introducing basic functional groups—such as quaternary ammonium groups ($-N^+R_3OH^-$) or tertiary amine groups—onto the styrene–divinylbenzene copolymer backbone and treating the polymer with dilute $NaOH$. In the hydroxide form, they release $OH^-$ ions in exchange for anions in the water.

![Chemical structure of quaternary ammonium styrene-divinylbenzene copolymer with N+R3 OH- groups](/notes/cse/s1/chemistry/module-4/assets/imagen-quaternary-ammonium-resin.png)
*Figure 4.2: Structure of a quaternary ammonium styrene–divinylbenzene anion-exchange resin with fixed $-N^+R_3$ groups and mobile $OH^-$ counter-ions.*

### The two-column demineralization process

Complete deionization is carried out by passing raw hard water sequentially through two packed resin columns.

![Schematic diagram of a two-column ion-exchange demineralization unit showing hard water entering the cation exchange bed, then the anion exchange bed, with acid and alkaline regenerators](/notes/cse/s1/chemistry/module-4/assets/page-011-image-1.png)
*Figure 4.3: Two-stage ion-exchange demineralization unit showing the cation-exchange bed followed by the anion-exchange bed and their respective acid and alkali regeneration lines.*

1. **Passage through the cation-exchange column (CER):**
   Hard water enters the first column packed with $RH$ resin. All dissolved cations ($Ca^{2+}, Mg^{2+}, Na^+$) are retained by the resin, releasing an equivalent amount of $H^+$ ions into the water:
   $$
   2RH + Ca^{2+} \longrightarrow R_2Ca + 2H^+
   $$
   $$
   2RH + Mg^{2+} \longrightarrow R_2Mg + 2H^+
   $$
   Written in molecular form for dissolved calcium chloride ($CaCl_2$), the exchange produces dilute hydrochloric acid:
   $$
   2RH + CaCl_2 \longrightarrow R_2Ca + 2HCl
   $$
   Similarly, sulphate salts are converted into dilute sulphuric acid ($H_2SO_4$). Consequently, the effluent leaving the cation bed is free of metallic cations but is distinctly acidic ($\text{pH} < 7$).

2. **Passage through the anion-exchange column (AER):**
   The acidic, cation-free water is next fed into the second column containing $R'OH$ resin. Here, anions such as $Cl^-$ and $SO_4^{2-}$ are exchanged for an equivalent quantity of $OH^-$ ions:
   $$
   R'OH + Cl^- \longrightarrow R'Cl + OH^-
   $$
   $$
   2R'OH + SO_4^{2-} \longrightarrow (R')_2SO_4 + 2OH^-
   $$
   Because the incoming water contains free mineral acids ($HCl$, $H_2SO_4$) formed in the first column, the overall reactions in the second column neutralize the acids directly to water:
   $$
   R'OH + HCl \longrightarrow R'Cl + H_2O
   $$
   $$
   2R'OH + H_2SO_4 \longrightarrow (R')_2SO_4 + 2H_2O
   $$

3. **Neutralization and deionization:**
   The $H^+$ ions released in the first column and the $OH^-$ ions released in the second column combine rapidly:
   $$
   H^+ + OH^- \longrightarrow H_2O
   $$
   The water emerging from the second unit has very low dissolved mineral content when the resins are operating properly. Residual ions, dissolved gases and operating conditions affect its conductivity and pH. This product is called **demineralized** or **deionized water**.

#### Why does hard water pass through the cation column first?
In standard two-bed demineralization, feeding raw hard water directly into the hydroxide-form anion exchanger ($R'OH$) is avoided. If salts such as $MgCl_2$ or $CaSO_4$ encounter a high concentration of $OH^-$ ions before divalent cations are removed, insoluble metal hydroxides such as magnesium hydroxide precipitate immediately inside the resin bed:
$$
2R'OH + MgCl_2 \longrightarrow 2R'Cl + Mg(OH)_2\downarrow
$$
This solid precipitate coats the resin beads and clogs the interstices of the column, halting ion exchange. Removing $Ca^{2+}$ and $Mg^{2+}$ in the acidic cation bed first keeps all species in solution. (Note that while specialized industrial configurations like mixed-bed polishers or anion-first softeners for specific low-hardness waters exist, pre-removal of polyvalent cations is essential in standard two-bed hard-water demineralizers.)

### Regeneration of exhausted beds

After continuous operation, all mobile $H^+$ and $OH^-$ counter-ions on the resins are replaced by raw-water ions; the beds are then said to be **exhausted**. Because ion exchange is an equilibrium process, passing a concentrated solution of acid or alkali reverses the exchange reactions and restores the resins to their active forms:

1. **Regeneration of the cation exchanger:**
   Flushing the exhausted bed with dilute hydrochloric acid ($HCl$) or dilute sulphuric acid ($H_2SO_4$) drives $Ca^{2+}$ and $Mg^{2+}$ off the polymer matrix:
   $$
   R_2Ca + 2HCl \longrightarrow 2RH + CaCl_2\text{ (washings)}
   $$
2. **Regeneration of the anion exchanger:**
   Flushing the exhausted bed with dilute sodium hydroxide ($NaOH$) restores the hydroxide counter-ions:
   $$
   R'Cl + NaOH \longrightarrow R'OH + NaCl\text{ (washings)}
   $$
   $$
   (R')_2SO_4 + 2NaOH \longrightarrow 2R'OH + Na_2SO_4\text{ (washings)}
   $$

The soluble regeneration by-products ($CaCl_2$, $NaCl$, $Na_2SO_4$) are drained to waste, and the beds are rinsed with deionized water before returning to service.

### Merits and limitations

* **Advantages:**
  * Can treat a range of feed-water compositions when resin type and pretreatment suit the feed.
  * Produces water with extremely low residual hardness and very low electrical conductivity, making it useful in preparing high-purity water for applications such as boiler feed and lead–acid battery topping-up, subject to the application’s specifications.
  * Generates no bulky precipitate sludge during normal operation, and the resins can be regenerated and reused over many cycles.
* **Limitations:**
  * Capital cost of the ion-exchange columns, resins, and regeneration chemicals ($HCl$, $NaOH$) is high.
  * **Pretreatment requirement:** Turbid water containing suspended solids, colloidal clay, oil, or dissolved iron ($Fe^{2+}$) and manganese fouls the micropores of the resin beads, blocking active sites and drastically lowering exchange efficiency. Turbid feeds must be coagulated and filtered before entering the resin beds.

### Self-check questions

1. **Why is the effluent from the first column of a two-bed demineralizer acidic, and how is this acidity eliminated in the second column?**
   *Answer:* In the cation-exchange column ($RH$), every divalent cation ($Ca^{2+}, Mg^{2+}$) removed from solution releases two $H^+$ ions, converting dissolved salts ($CaCl_2, MgSO_4$) into equivalent mineral acids ($HCl, H_2SO_4$). In the anion-exchange column ($R'OH$), the anions ($Cl^-, SO_4^{2-}$) are exchanged for $OH^-$ ions, which immediately neutralize the $H^+$ ions to form $H_2O$.

2. **A student claims that passing hard water through a sodium-form cation resin ($RNa$) produces deionized water suitable for a high-pressure boiler. Critique this statement.**
   *Answer:* The claim is incorrect. An $RNa$ resin replaces $Ca^{2+}$ and $Mg^{2+}$ with $Na^+$ ions ($2RNa + Ca^{2+} \rightarrow R_2Ca + 2Na^+$). While this softens the water, the effluent retains an equivalent concentration of dissolved sodium salts ($NaCl, Na_2SO_4, NaHCO_3$), so it is still conductive and contains dissolved minerals rather than being deionized.

## Reverse osmosis and desalination

Desalination is the process of removing excess dissolved salts and minerals from brackish water or seawater to produce fresh water suitable for domestic or industrial use. Unlike ion-exchange softening, which is uneconomical for high-salinity waters due to rapid resin exhaustion, membrane-based reverse osmosis (RO) is widely used to desalinate saline feedwater.

### Osmosis and osmotic pressure

When two aqueous solutions of different solute concentrations are separated by a semipermeable membrane (which selectively allows water molecules to pass while rejecting most dissolved ions and larger molecules), water naturally flows from the dilute solution (higher water chemical potential) to the concentrated solution (lower water chemical potential). This spontaneous net movement of solvent is called **natural osmosis**.

The hydrostatic pressure difference that builds up across the membrane at equilibrium—or the minimum excess pressure that must be applied to the concentrated side just to halt this net solvent flow—is the **osmotic pressure** ($\pi$, typically measured in $\text{bar}$ or $\text{MPa}$). For dilute ideal solutions, osmotic pressure increases with molar concentration $C$ of the solute before dissociation and absolute temperature $T$ according to the van 't Hoff relation:
$$
\pi = iCRT
$$
where $i$ is the van 't Hoff factor and $R$ is the universal gas constant.

### Principle of reverse osmosis

If an external hydrostatic pressure is applied to the concentrated saline side, the direction of water transport depends on the applied transmembrane pressure difference ($\Delta P$) relative to the osmotic pressure difference ($\Delta \pi$) between the concentrated feed and the dilute product:

* **$\Delta P < \Delta \pi$:** Natural osmosis occurs; water flows from the dilute side to the concentrated side.
* **$\Delta P = \Delta \pi$:** Osmotic equilibrium is reached; there is no net flow of water across the membrane.
* **$\Delta P > \Delta \pi$:** **Reverse osmosis** occurs; water is forced in the reverse direction, from the concentrated saline feed into the lower-salinity permeate stream.

> **Watch out.** Do not compare osmotic pressure $\pi$ simply to absolute atmospheric pressure $P$. Reverse osmosis requires the applied **transmembrane pressure difference** ($\Delta P$) to exceed the osmotic pressure difference ($\Delta \pi$) plus hydraulic friction and membrane resistance losses so that a practical permeate flux is achieved.

![Schematic of reverse osmosis showing external pressure applied via a piston to seawater, forcing water molecules through a semipermeable membrane into the fresh water compartment while rejecting salts.](/notes/cse/s1/chemistry/module-4/assets/page-015-image-2-Imagen.png)
*Figure: Principle of reverse osmosis—applying a pressure greater than the osmotic pressure forces water from the concentrated salt solution through a semipermeable membrane.*

### Process steps and membrane materials

An industrial RO desalination unit operates as a continuous cross-flow filtration system rather than a dead-end batch piston:

1. **Pretreatment:** Raw seawater or brackish water is screened, coagulated, filtered, and chemically conditioned (for example, with antiscalants and dechlorination agents) to remove suspended solids, biological growth, and free chlorine that would otherwise foul or degrade the membrane.
2. **Pressurization:** A high-pressure feed pump raises the pretreated feedwater pressure well above its osmotic pressure (with the required pressure depending on salinity, membrane characteristics and recovery).
3. **Membrane separation:** Pressurized feedwater enters the membrane module. Semi-permeable membranes used in educational and industrial contexts include **cellulose acetate** and **thin-film composite aromatic polyamide** (often miswritten as polyimide in handwritten notes) membranes. Water passes through the membrane to form the low-salinity **permeate** (product stream), while rejected salts, organics, and colloids remain in the pressurized **concentrate** (reject or brine stream).
4. **Post-treatment and brine management:** The permeate is stabilized (pH and mineral adjustment) and disinfected, while the high-salinity concentrate is routed to energy-recovery devices and discharged or treated.

### Advantages and limitations of reverse osmosis

* **Broad contaminant rejection:** Rejects a high fraction of dissolved ionic salts alongside non-ionic contaminants such as dissolved organic matter, bacteria, and **colloidal silica** (which passes through standard ion-exchange beds).
* **No thermal phase change:** Because water does not need to be vaporized, RO often has a lower energy requirement than thermal desalination; comparisons depend on feedwater, recovery and energy integration.
* **Continuous operation:** Does not require periodic acid/alkali chemical regeneration cycles like ion-exchange beds, though periodic chemical cleaning in place (CIP) is needed.

> **Watch out.** Reverse osmosis does not universally remove a fixed $99\%$ or $100\%$ of all ions, nor does it always have low capital cost. Rejection depends on membrane type, ion charge/size, feed salinity, and operating conditions, and high-pressure pumps, pretreatment systems, and membranes represent significant capital and electrical operating costs.

**Key limitations:**
* **High electrical energy demand:** High-pressure pumps consume considerable electricity, especially for seawater.
* **Membrane fouling and scaling:** Suspended particulates, biofilms, and sparingly soluble salts ($\text{CaCO}_3$, $\text{CaSO}_4$, silica) can foul or scale the membrane surface, reducing permeate flux and membrane lifespan.
* **Concentrate (brine) disposal:** Discharging large volumes of hyper-saline reject stream poses environmental challenges for marine or inland ecosystems.

### Comparison: Ion exchange vs. Reverse osmosis

| Feature | Ion-exchange demineralization | Reverse osmosis (RO) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Primary driving force** | Chemical exchange of ions on functional resin sites | Applied transmembrane pressure ($\Delta P > \Delta \pi$) |
| **Contaminants removed** | Dissolved ionic species only | Dissolved ions, organics, colloids, and colloidal silica |
| **Best suited feedwater** | Low-to-moderate total dissolved solids (TDS) | Brackish water, seawater, or moderate-to-high TDS water |
| **Main waste stream** | Acidic and alkaline spent regeneration effluents | Continuous concentrated saline brine (reject) |

### Self-check questions

1. **Question:** Why is reverse osmosis able to remove colloidal silica from boiler feedwater whereas an ion-exchange demineralizer cannot?
   **Answer:** Colloidal silica is non-ionic (uncharged aggregate particles), so it does not undergo electrostatic ion exchange on cation or anion resins, whereas an RO membrane physically rejects it by size exclusion and solution-diffusion barriers.
2. **Question:** What happens in an RO cell if the applied transmembrane pressure equals the osmotic pressure difference across the membrane?
   **Answer:** The system is at osmotic equilibrium ($\Delta P = \Delta \pi$), so no net flow of water occurs in either direction.

## Water purification, chlorination and breakpoint

Natural waters may contain suspended solids, colloids, dissolved contaminants and pathogenic microorganisms. Treatment is selected according to raw-water quality and the intended use. Municipal water purification therefore uses a sequential treatment train. First, physical and chemical clarification steps remove turbidity and suspended matter; second, a disinfection stage inactivates waterborne pathogens before distribution.

### Municipal water purification train

Although the exact unit order can be adapted to raw-water quality, a conventional municipal treatment plant operates through the following stages:

1. **Screening:** Raw water passes through coarse and fine bar screens to trap floating debris such as leaves, twigs, plastics, and rags that would otherwise damage downstream pumps and clog pipes.
2. **Aeration:** Water is brought into intimate contact with air using spray nozzles or cascade aerators. Aeration strips out dissolved gases responsible for unpleasant tastes and odours (such as $\text{H}_2\text{S}$ and volatile organic compounds), increases dissolved oxygen, and oxidizes soluble iron ($\text{Fe}^{2+}$) and manganese ($\text{Mn}^{2+}$) into insoluble hydrous oxides that can be filtered out.
3. **Coagulation and flocculation:** Fine clay and colloidal particles (typically $1\text{ nm}$ to $1\text{ }\mu\text{m}$) do not settle under gravity alone because they carry negative surface charges that cause mutual electrostatic repulsion. Adding a chemical coagulant such as alum, $\text{Al}_2(\text{SO}_4)_3$, introduces trivalent $\text{Al}^{3+}$ ions that neutralize these surface charges. In water, alum undergoes hydrolysis to form a gelatinous precipitate of aluminium hydroxide:

$$
\text{Al}_2(\text{SO}_4)_3 + 6\text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow 2\text{Al(OH)}_3\downarrow + 3\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4
$$

   This schematic equation shows that alum hydrolysis generates protons (represented as $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$), which consume natural water alkalinity—primarily bicarbonate ions ($\text{HCO}_3^-$)—to keep the pH in the range where $\text{Al(OH)}_3$ precipitates. Gentle mechanical stirring (flocculation) then encourages the neutralized colloids to collide and stick to the sticky $\text{Al(OH)}_3$ flocs, growing into larger, heavier aggregates.
4. **Sedimentation:** The flocculated water flows slowly through large clarifying basins where it is held relatively undisturbed for several hours. Gravity pulls the heavy flocs and suspended silt to the tank floor as sludge, clarifying the bulk water.
5. **Filtration:** Clarified water percolates through graded beds of fine sand and gravel (gravity sand filters). Filtration captures remaining fine flocs, silt, and some larger protozoan cysts, producing visibly clear water. However, physical filtration alone cannot trap all bacteria and viruses; a suitable disinfection barrier is needed in this conventional treatment train.
6. **Disinfection:** The final stage destroys or inactivates disease-causing microorganisms (pathogens) rather than sterilizing the water of every living spore. Municipal plants use chemical disinfectants such as chlorine, hypochlorites, chloramines or ozone, or physical methods such as ultraviolet (UV) radiation.

### Chemistry of chlorination

Chlorination is the most widely used municipal disinfection method because it is economical, effective across a broad temperature range, and leaves a measurable protective residual in the distribution network. Chlorine may be applied as compressed $\text{Cl}_2$ gas using a chlorinator, or dosed as hypochlorite salts.

When molecular chlorine dissolves in water , it rapidly disproportionates in a reversible hydrolysis reaction to form hypochlorous acid ($\text{HOCl}$) and hydrochloric acid:

$$
\text{Cl}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightleftharpoons \text{HOCl} + \text{H}^+ + \text{Cl}^-
$$

Hypochlorous acid is a weak acid ($\text{p}K_a \approx 7.5$ at $25\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$) that partially dissociates into hydrogen ions and hypochlorite ions ($\text{OCl}^-$):

$$
\text{HOCl} \rightleftharpoons \text{H}^+ + \text{OCl}^-
$$

The sum of dissolved $\text{Cl}_2$, $\text{HOCl}$, and $\text{OCl}^-$ is termed **free available chlorine**. Because the second equilibrium depends directly on $\text{H}^+$ concentration, the disinfection power of chlorine is strongly pH-dependent:

- **Below $\text{pH } 7.5$ :** The uncharged $\text{HOCl}$ molecule predominates. Because $\text{HOCl}$ is electrically neutral and small, it easily penetrates the negatively charged cell walls of bacteria and inactivates essential metabolic enzymes by oxidizing sulfhydryl ($\text{-SH}$) and amino groups.
- **Above $\text{pH } 7.5$:** The equilibrium shifts toward the negatively charged $\text{OCl}^-$ ion, which is electrostatically repelled by negatively charged pathogen membranes and is a much slower disinfectant than $\text{HOCl}$.

> **Watch out.** Older textbooks often write $\text{HOCl} \longrightarrow \text{HCl} + [\text{O}]$ and attribute disinfection to "nascent oxygen" ($[\text{O}]$). That is an obsolete shorthand cartoon, not the real chemical mechanism. Hypochlorous acid ($\text{HOCl}$) is the direct active oxidizing and chlorinating agent that damages microbial enzymes and cell membranes.

#### Hypochlorites and bleaching powder

To avoid the transport and leak hazards of pressurized $\text{Cl}_2$ gas, smaller plants often dose liquid sodium hypochlorite ($\text{NaOCl}$) or solid calcium-based chlorinating agents. Commercial **bleaching powder** is not a pure compound; it is a complex mixture containing calcium hypochlorite, $\text{Ca(OCl)}_2$, basic calcium chloride, and free slaked lime, $\text{Ca(OH)}_2$, often represented by the simplified empirical formula $\text{Ca(OCl)Cl}$.

When calcium hypochlorite dissolves in water, it dissociates to release hypochlorite ions, which then protonate to generate active hypochlorous acid:

$$
\text{Ca(OCl)}_2 \longrightarrow \text{Ca}^{2+} + 2\text{OCl}^-
$$

$$
\text{OCl}^- + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightleftharpoons \text{HOCl} + \text{OH}^-
$$

> **Watch out.** Do not write $\text{CaOCl}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow \text{Ca(OH)}_2 + \text{Cl}_2$. Adding bleaching powder to neutral water yields aqueous calcium, chloride, and hypochlorite/hypochlorous acid species—the active disinfectant is described by the hypochlorite–hypochlorous acid equilibria.

Using bleaching powder has practical drawbacks compared to $\text{Cl}_2$ gas or $\text{NaOCl}$: it decomposes during prolonged storage via moisture and $\text{CO}_2$ absorption, its $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ and $\text{OH}^-$ content increases water hardness and pH, and overdosing produces an unpleasant taste and odour.

### Chlorine demand, residuals, and breakpoint chlorination

When chlorine is added to raw water, part of the dose is consumed by reactions with inorganic reducing agents, ammonia, and dissolved organic matter before any remains to protect the supply. For a defined contact time, temperature, and pH, the relationship is:

$$
\text{Chlorine dose} = \text{Chlorine demand} + \text{Chlorine residual}
$$

where all three terms are typically measured in $\text{mg/L}$ (or $\text{ppm}$) as $\text{Cl}_2$.

In waters containing dissolved ammonia ($\text{NH}_3$ or $\text{NH}_4^+$), hypochlorous acid reacts stepwise to substitute hydrogen atoms with chlorine, forming **chloramines** (known collectively as **combined available chlorine**):

$$
\text{NH}_3 + \text{HOCl} \longrightarrow \underset{\text{monochloramine}}{\text{NH}_2\text{Cl}} + \text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

$$
\text{NH}_2\text{Cl} + \text{HOCl} \longrightarrow \underset{\text{dichloramine}}{\text{NHCl}_2} + \text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

$$
\text{NHCl}_2 + \text{HOCl} \longrightarrow \underset{\text{trichloramine}}{\text{NCl}_3} + \text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

Monochloramine is a slower disinfectant than free $\text{HOCl}$, fails to oxidize many odour-causing compounds, and should not be represented as simply hydrolyzing back to $\text{HOCl}$ to disinfect. However, because it is more stable and less reactive with organic matter, utilities sometimes intentionally form monochloramine as a long-lasting secondary residual in long pipe networks.

If increasing doses of chlorine are added to water containing reducing agents and ammonia, the measured total residual chlorine follows a characteristic four-stage curve known as the **breakpoint chlorination curve**.

![Breakpoint chlorination curve plotting residual chlorine against applied chlorine dose across four distinct reaction zones](/notes/cse/s1/chemistry/module-4/assets/imagen-breakpoint-chlorination.png)
*Figure: Breakpoint chlorination curve showing how measured chlorine residual changes with applied chlorine dose across four reaction stages.*

| Curve region | Dominant chemical process | Residual behaviour |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Stage I** (initial demand) | Fast reduction of chlorine to inert $\text{Cl}^-$ by strong inorganic reducing agents ($\text{Fe}^{2+}$, $\text{Mn}^{2+}$, $\text{H}_2\text{S}$, $\text{NO}_2^-$). | Virtually zero residual appears. |
| **Stage II** (combined rise) | Reaction of $\text{HOCl}$ with ammonia and organic amines to form monochloramine ($\text{NH}_2\text{Cl}$) and chloro-organic compounds. | Measured residual rises steadily as combined chlorine. |
| **Stage III** (chloramine destruction) | Excess $\text{HOCl}$ oxidizes chloramines and some nitrogenous compounds to products including nitrogen gas ($\text{N}_2\uparrow$), nitrate and chloride, depending on conditions. | Measured residual drops sharply toward a minimum. |
| **Stage IV** (post-breakpoint) | Most ammonia-related demand and rapid reducing demand have been satisfied; further additions remain as unreacted $\text{HOCl}$ and $\text{OCl}^-$. | Free chlorine residual rises linearly with applied dose. |

The dip at the end of Stage III is the **breakpoint**. Operating beyond the breakpoint can leave a controllable **free chlorine residual** ($\text{HOCl}/\text{OCl}^-$) for rapid pathogen inactivation. Note, however, that breakpoint chlorination does not oxidize *all* natural organic matter to $\text{CO}_2$; instead, excess free chlorine can react with humic substances to form unwanted chlorinated disinfection by-products.

### Dechlorination and operational controls

Over-chlorination well beyond the breakpoint imparts a pungent medicinal taste and odour and irritates mucous membranes. When a process requires chlorine removal, **dechlorination** can be used either by passing the water through beds of granular activated carbon (GAC) or by dosing reducing sulphur compounds such as sulphur dioxide ($\text{SO}_2$), sodium sulphite ($\text{Na}_2\text{SO}_3$), or sodium thiosulphate ($\text{Na}_2\text{S}_2\text{O}_3$):

$$
\text{SO}_2 + \text{HOCl} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow \text{SO}_4^{2-} + \text{Cl}^- + 3\text{H}^+
$$

$$
\text{SO}_3^{2-} + \text{HOCl} \longrightarrow \text{SO}_4^{2-} + \text{Cl}^- + \text{H}^+
$$

Effective chlorination cannot rely on a single fixed dose or contact time for all waters. Engineers must control four operational variables simultaneously:

- **Contact time and temperature:** Disinfection rate increases with temperature; colder water requires longer contact times or higher residuals.
- **pH control:** Lower pH favours the more effective $\text{HOCl}$ species. Operating pH must also account for corrosion control, alkalinity and the wider treatment process.
- **Turbidity removal:** Upstream coagulation and filtration are essential because suspended particles shield embedded bacteria from $\text{HOCl}$.
- **By-product management:** Avoiding excessive post-breakpoint doses limits the formation of regulated chlorinated organic by-products.

### Self-check questions

1. **Question:** Why does the disinfecting efficiency of free chlorine drop significantly when the water pH rises from $6.5$ to $8.5$?
   **Answer:** Because hypochlorous acid ($\text{HOCl}$, $\text{p}K_a \approx 7.5$) dissociates into $\text{H}^+$ and $\text{OCl}^-$ at higher pH. Neutral $\text{HOCl}$ penetrates negatively charged microbial cell membranes rapidly, whereas the negatively charged $\text{OCl}^-$ ion is electrostatically repelled and acts much more slowly.

2. **Question:** During breakpoint chlorination, why does the measured total chlorine residual *decrease* as more chlorine is added in Stage III?
   **Answer:** In Stage III, additional hypochlorous acid oxidizes the previously formed chloramines (combined chlorine) primarily into nitrogen gas ($\text{N}_2$) and chloride ions ($\text{Cl}^-$), neither of which registers as active chlorine residual.

## Ozone and ultraviolet disinfection

Disinfection is the selective inactivation of disease-causing microorganisms (pathogens) to safe levels, whereas sterilization is the complete destruction of all living microbes and spores. Municipal water treatment targets disinfection rather than full sterilization. While chlorination is widely used, alternative disinfectants like ozone ($\text{O}_3$) and ultraviolet (UV) radiation avoid chlorinated taste and odour issues.

### Ozone disinfection

Ozone is an unstable, strongly oxidizing gas. Because it decomposes rapidly and is unsuitable for routine bulk storage, it must be generated on-site by passing dry, cold oxygen or air through a high-voltage silent electric discharge:

$$
3\text{O}_2(\text{g}) \xrightarrow{\text{electric discharge}} 2\text{O}_3(\text{g})
$$

In treatment plants, the ozonized gas is bubbled into water within a contact chamber to ensure efficient gas-to-liquid mass transfer. Older textbooks often write a simplified decomposition into molecular oxygen and an oxygen atom ($\text{O}_3 \rightarrow \text{O}_2 + [\text{O}]$). In aqueous solution, however, disinfection actually proceeds through two concurrent chemical pathways:

1. **Direct oxidation** by dissolved molecular ozone ($\text{O}_3(\text{aq})$).
2. **Indirect oxidation** by highly reactive hydroxyl radicals ($\cdot\text{OH}$) formed when ozone decomposes in water.

These strong oxidants rupture microbial cell walls, oxidize nucleic acids, and break down many oxidizable organic compounds responsible for unpleasant tastes, odours, and colour. However, ozonation does not remove dissolved inorganic salts or non-oxidizable pollutants.

> **Watch out.** Do not refer to $[\text{O}]$ as "nascent $\text{O}_2$"—$\text{O}_2$ is molecular dioxygen, and aqueous ozonation relies on dissolved $\text{O}_3$ and hydroxyl radicals ($\cdot\text{OH}$), not isolated atomic oxygen alone.

**Merits and limitations of ozonation:**
* **Merits:** Rapidly inactivates bacteria, viruses, and chlorine-resistant protozoan cysts; removes oxidizable tastes and odours without adding persistent foreign ions.
* **Limitations:** High capital and electrical energy costs for on-site generation; if the source water contains bromide ions ($\text{Br}^-$), ozone can oxidize them into carcinogenic bromate ($\text{BrO}_3^-$). Furthermore, because $\text{O}_3$ decomposes quickly back to $\text{O}_2$, it leaves **no lasting residual disinfectant** in the distribution pipes to guard against post-treatment recontamination.

### Ultraviolet (UV) disinfection

Ultraviolet disinfection is a physical, chemical-free process. While the broad UV spectrum spans $100\text{–}400\text{ nm}$, not all UV wavelengths are equally effective. Water disinfection commonly uses **UV-C radiation**, especially the $254\text{ nm}$ emission of low-pressure mercury lamps; effectiveness varies with wavelength and organism. In practice, water flows in thin layers past low-pressure mercury-vapour lamps enclosed in protective **quartz sleeves** (quartz transmits UV-C radiation, whereas ordinary glass absorbs it).

Instead of chemically oxidizing the cell wall, $254\text{ nm}$ photons penetrate the microorganism and are absorbed by cellular DNA and RNA. This energy causes adjacent pyrimidine bases (such as thymine) to form covalent dimers. The damaged genetic material prevents cellular replication, rendering the pathogen harmless without physically removing it from the water.

> **Watch out.** Conventional UV disinfection does not remove dissolved solids, hardness or heavy metals, and it only inactivates microbes when they receive a sufficient light dose.

**Operational requirements and limitations:**
* **Water clarity:** Suspended particles (turbidity) shield microbes from photons, and dissolved organic matter or iron absorbs UV light. Water must be pre-filtered to high clarity before UV exposure.
* **Lamp maintenance:** Mineral scaling or biofilm fouling on the quartz sleeve blocks UV transmission, requiring regular cleaning.
* **Dose and contact time:** Flow rate must be controlled so all water receives the required UV fluence (intensity $\times$ exposure time).
* **No residual:** Like ozone, UV treatment provides zero residual protection once water leaves the reactor.

### Comparison of disinfection methods

| Feature | Chlorination ($\text{Cl}_2 / \text{HOCl}$) | Ozonation ($\text{O}_3$) | UV irradiation (UV-C, $\approx 254\text{ nm}$) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Primary agent** | Hypochlorous acid ($\text{HOCl}$) | Dissolved $\text{O}_3$ and $\cdot\text{OH}$ radicals | Germicidal photons (physical agent) |
| **Mechanism** | Chemical oxidation of cellular enzymes and structures | Chemical oxidation of cell walls and nucleic acids | Photochemical dimerization of DNA/RNA; stops replication |
| **Distribution residual** | Yes (free or combined chlorine residual) | None (decomposes rapidly to $\text{O}_2$) | None (acts only inside the lamp chamber) |
| **Key merits** | Economical; provides ongoing pipe protection | Very strong oxidant; destroys cysts and oxidizable odours | No disinfectant added; generally little effect on taste and odour |
| **Key limitations** | Can form chlorophenols and trihalomethanes; taste issues | High energy/equipment cost; on-site generation; bromate risk | Requires clear water and clean quartz sleeves; no residual |

### Self-check questions

1. **Why must water undergo filtration to remove turbidity before entering a UV disinfection unit?**
   *Answer:* Suspended particles scatter and absorb UV-C photons, shielding embedded microorganisms from the radiation needed to damage their DNA.
2. **Why do large municipal networks often add a small dose of chlorine after ozonation or UV treatment?**
   *Answer:* Neither ozone nor UV leaves a persistent disinfectant residual in the water; a small secondary chlorine dose protects the water against microbial regrowth or contamination inside long distribution pipelines.

## Water pollution, dissolved oxygen, BOD and COD

### Sources and Types of Water Pollution
Water pollution occurs when unwanted substances enter water bodies and degrade their quality for aquatic ecosystems or human use. Major sources fall into four categories:

1. **Domestic sewage:** Wastewater from households and municipal drains carries biodegradable organic matter, detergents, and waterborne **pathogens** (bacteria, viruses, and protozoa).
2. **Industrial effluents:** Untreated or poorly treated factory discharges can introduce synthetic **organics** (solvents, dyes, phenols), acids, alkalis, and toxic heavy **metals** (such as lead, mercury, cadmium, and chromium). Properly treated industrial effluents do not necessarily pollute.
3. **Agricultural runoff:** Rainwater washing over farmland carries dissolved plant **nutrients** (nitrates and phosphates from fertilizers) and persistent pesticides into rivers and lakes. Excess nutrients trigger eutrophication—rapid algal blooms that eventually decay and strip the water of oxygen.
4. **Thermal discharges:** Cooling water released from power plants and factories raises the ambient temperature of receiving waters, directly lowering oxygen solubility and accelerating aquatic metabolic rates.

### Aerobic vs. Anaerobic Decomposition
Microorganisms break down organic pollutants in sewage through two distinct pathways depending on whether free dissolved oxygen is present.

| Feature | Aerobic Decomposition | Anaerobic Decomposition |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Oxygen requirement** | Occurs in the presence of free dissolved $\text{O}_2$. | Occurs in the complete absence of free dissolved $\text{O}_2$. |
| **Microorganisms** | Driven by aerobic bacteria. | Driven by anaerobic microorganisms, including bacteria and methanogenic archaea. |
| **Typical end products** | $\text{CO}_2$, $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, nitrates ($\text{NO}_3^-$), sulphates ($\text{SO}_4^{2-}$), phosphates ($\text{PO}_4^{3-}$), and new biomass. | Methane ($\text{CH}_4$), $\text{CO}_2$, organic acids (e.g., acetic acid), ammonia ($\text{NH}_3$), hydrogen sulphide ($\text{H}_2\text{S}$), and biomass. |
| **Odour** | Usually less odorous when well controlled. | Can produce offensive odours due to $\text{H}_2\text{S}$ and $\text{NH}_3$). |
| **Energy and biogas** | Releases more energy per mole of substrate; no biogas fuel is produced. | Releases less energy; generates methane-rich biogas fuel. |

Using glucose ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6$) as a model carbohydrate, the simplified reactions are:

$$
\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 + 6\text{O}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{Aerobic bacteria}} 6\text{CO}_2 + 6\text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

$$
\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 \xrightarrow{\text{Anaerobic bacteria}} 3\text{CH}_4 + 3\text{CO}_2
$$

> **Watch out.** Products depend on the substrate and microbial community. Complete nitrification produces nitrate, but nitrite and other intermediates can occur. Methanogenic anaerobic digestion operates without free molecular oxygen; sulphate-reducing microbes can produce foul-smelling $\text{H}_2\text{S}$.

### Dissolved Oxygen (DO)
**Dissolved oxygen (DO)** is the concentration of free, non-compound molecular oxygen ($\text{O}_2$) dissolved in water, expressed in milligrams per litre ($\text{mg/L}$ or $\text{ppm}$). It refers strictly to dissolved $\text{O}_2$ gas, not the oxygen atoms chemically bound within $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ molecules. DO supports fish and aerobic microbes; its concentration varies with temperature, pressure, salinity and biological activity. DO is measured in the laboratory using **Winkler's iodometric titration** or directly in the field with an electrochemical or optical DO meter.

Four main factors govern DO levels in natural waters:
* **Temperature:** Gas solubility in water is exothermic. As water temperature increases, DO solubility decreases; cold water holds significantly more $\text{O}_2$ than warm water.
* **Atmospheric pressure and aeration:** By Henry's law, gas solubility rises with partial pressure. Water at high altitudes experiences lower atmospheric pressure and therefore holds less DO. Turbulence, wind, and artificial aeration increase the rate of $\text{O}_2$ transfer into water.
* **Salinity:** Dissolved salts reduce oxygen solubility through the salting-out effect, so seawater holds less DO than freshwater at the same temperature.
* **Biological oxygen demand:** When biodegradable organic pollutants (such as starch, cellulose, or sewage) enter water, aerobic bacteria multiply and consume DO faster than atmospheric re-aeration can replace it, causing DO levels to plummet.

### Biochemical Oxygen Demand ($\text{BOD}_5$)
**Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD)** is the mass of dissolved oxygen consumed by aerobic microorganisms during biochemical oxidation in one litre of water, including biodegradable organic matter and, when not inhibited, nitrification under standard conditions: **$20^\circ\text{C}$ in the dark for $5\text{ days}$** (often written as $\text{BOD}_5$). Darkness prevents photosynthetic algae in the sample from generating extra oxygen during incubation.

$\text{BOD}_5$ is an empirical $5\text{-day}$ test, not a measure of complete (ultimate) biological oxidation, nor is it a direct count of bacteria. A high BOD indicates heavy biodegradable organic pollution and warns that discharging the waste directly into a river will deplete ambient DO and suffocate aquatic life.

#### Example 1: Additional illustrative example — Calculating $\text{BOD}_5$ from a diluted sewage sample
**Problem:** Because raw sewage contains far more organic matter than the $6\text{–}9\text{ mg/L}$ of oxygen available in saturated water, samples must be diluted before incubation. Suppose $6\text{ mL}$ of municipal wastewater is diluted to $300\text{ mL}$ in a standard BOD bottle using oxygen-saturated dilution water (volumetric dilution fraction $P = 0.02$). The initial dissolved oxygen ($\text{DO}_i$) is $8.0\text{ mg/L}$, and after $5\text{ days}$ at $20^\circ\text{C}$, the final dissolved oxygen ($\text{DO}_f$) is $3.0\text{ mg/L}$. Calculate the $\text{BOD}_5$ of the wastewater.

*Assumptions:* The dilution water is unseeded (no seed correction required), and no nitrification inhibitor is added.

**Solution:**
The volumetric fraction $P$ of wastewater in the bottle is:

$$
P = \frac{\text{Volume of undiluted sample}}{\text{Total diluted volume}} = \frac{6\text{ mL}}{300\text{ mL}} = 0.02
$$

For an unseeded dilution, $\text{BOD}_5$ is the oxygen depletion divided by the dilution fraction $P$:

$$
\text{BOD}_5 = \frac{\text{DO}_i - \text{DO}_f}{P}
$$

Substitute $\text{DO}_i = 8.0\text{ mg/L}$, $\text{DO}_f = 3.0\text{ mg/L}$, and $P = 0.02$:

$$
\text{BOD}_5 = \frac{8.0\text{ mg/L} - 3.0\text{ mg/L}}{0.02} = \frac{5.0\text{ mg/L}}{0.02} = 250\text{ mg O}_2\text{/L}
$$

### Chemical Oxygen Demand (COD)
**Chemical Oxygen Demand (COD)** is the mass of oxygen equivalent to the amount of a strong chemical oxidant—standardly **potassium dichromate ($\text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7$) in boiling concentrated sulphuric acid**—consumed when oxidizing the oxidizable organic and inorganic matter in one litre of water under defined reflux conditions (typically $2\text{–}3\text{ hours}$).

During the test, orange $\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}$ ions are reduced to green $\text{Cr}^{3+}$ ions, and the unreacted dichromate is titrated to find how much oxidant was consumed, which is then converted into stoichiometric $\text{mg O}_2\text{/L}$. Even this harsh reagent does not oxidize every possible compound (some aromatic hydrocarbons resist attack), and dissolved **chloride ions ($\text{Cl}^-$) interfere** by being oxidized to $\text{Cl}_2$, artificially inflating the COD unless masked (commonly by adding mercuric sulphate, $\text{HgSO}_4$, to complex the chloride).

> **Watch out.** For a given wastewater, $\text{COD}$ is generally greater than or equal to $\text{BOD}_5$ because acidic dichromate oxidizes both biodegradable organics (like glucose) and biologically recalcitrant organics (such as some dyes and other slowly biodegradable compounds), as well as oxidizable inorganic ions ($\text{Fe}^{2+}$, $\text{S}^{2-}$). However, there is no universal mathematical conversion factor between COD and BOD; you cannot calculate BOD from COD unless an empirical correlation has already been established for that specific wastewater stream.

### Comparison of DO, $\text{BOD}_5$, and COD

| Parameter | What It Measures | Typical Units | Standard Test Time | Water Quality Significance |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **DO** | Free molecular $\text{O}_2$ physically dissolved in water at the moment of sampling. | $\text{mg O}_2\text{/L}$ ($\text{ppm}$) | Immediate (minutes) | Indicates immediate ability of water to support fish and aerobic microbes. *Note: High DO alone does not guarantee potability, because DO does not directly measure toxic metals, pesticides or pathogens.* |
| **$\text{BOD}_5$** | Oxygen consumed by aerobic microbes oxidizing biodegradable organic matter at $20^\circ\text{C}$. | $\text{mg O}_2\text{/L}$ ($\text{ppm}$) | $5\text{ days}$ | Measures biodegradable organic pollution load and potential DO depletion in receiving rivers. |
| **COD** | Oxygen equivalent of chemical oxidant ($\text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7\text{/H}_2\text{SO}_4$) consumed by chemically oxidizable matter. | $\text{mg O}_2\text{/L}$ ($\text{ppm}$) | $\approx 2\text{–}3\text{ hours}$ | Rapidly measures total chemically oxidizable load (both biodegradable and non-biodegradable). |

### Self-Check Questions
1. **Why does a water sample from a fast-flowing mountain stream at low altitude on a winter morning have a higher DO than a stagnant pond on a hot summer afternoon?**
   *Answer:* Cold water has a higher gas solubility for $\text{O}_2$ than warm water, low altitude provides higher atmospheric partial pressure of $\text{O}_2$, and fast-flowing turbulence promotes continuous atmospheric re-aeration.
2. **An industrial effluent containing synthetic dyes and cellulose shows $\text{COD} = 600\text{ mg/L}$ and $\text{BOD}_5 = 120\text{ mg/L}$. Why is the COD so much larger than the $\text{BOD}_5$?**
   *Answer:* Aerobic microbes in the $5\text{-day}$ BOD test can only break down the biodegradable fraction, whereas hot acidic $\text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7$ in the COD test chemically oxidizes both the biodegradable organic matter and the biologically resistant synthetic dyes (plus any slowly degrading cellulose).

## Sewage treatment: stages and activated sludge

Domestic and municipal wastewater (**sewage**) originates from households, institutions, and commercial buildings, carrying human waste, soaps, food scraps, and pathogenic microorganisms. It differs fundamentally from **industrial effluent**, which discharges from manufacturing or chemical plants and often contains heavy metals, synthetic solvents, extreme pH, or recalcitrant toxins requiring specialized pretreatment. Sewage treatment reduces Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD), suspended solids, nutrients, and pathogens before water is released into the environment or reused, preventing dissolved oxygen depletion and waterborne disease.

### Overview of Treatment Stages

Municipal sewage treatment progresses through physical, biological, and chemical/advanced stages:

**Raw Sewage** $\rightarrow$ **Preliminary Treatment** (Screening & Grit Removal) $\rightarrow$ **Primary Treatment** (Primary Sedimentation) $\rightarrow$ **Secondary Treatment** (Biological Conversion + Biomass Separation) $\rightarrow$ **Tertiary Treatment** (Nutrient Removal, Polishing & Disinfection) $\rightarrow$ **Regulated Discharge or Reuse**
*(Note: Settled solids from primary and secondary clarifiers are routed to **Sludge Processing**: Thickening $\rightarrow$ Anaerobic Digestion $\rightarrow$ Dewatering $\rightarrow$ Managed Disposal/Use.)*

| Stage | Core Mechanism | Primary Target Pollutants | Typical Operations |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Preliminary** | Physical interception | Rags, plastics, large debris, sand, gravel | Bar screens, mesh screens, grit chambers |
| **Primary** | Gravitational settling | Settleable suspended solids, floating grease/scum | Primary clarifiers (plain or chemically enhanced) |
| **Secondary** | Biological conversion | Dissolved and colloidal biodegradable organics (BOD) | Activated sludge or trickling filters with clarification; UASB with an integrated separator and suitable polishing |
| **Tertiary** | Advanced physical, chemical or biological | Phosphorus, nitrogen, residual suspended solids, pathogens | Chemical precipitation, nitrification/denitrification, filtration, disinfection |

### Preliminary and Primary Treatment

**Preliminary treatment** protects downstream pumps and pipes from clogging and abrasion.
1. **Screening:** Raw sewage passes through inclined bar screens and finer mesh screens to trap large floating and suspended debris (plastics, rags, leaves, wood). Because screenings consist largely of non-biodegradable trash, they are compacted and sent to landfills or incineration—not fed into biological reactors.
2. **Grit removal:** Velocity-controlled grit chambers allow dense inorganic particles (sand, silt, broken glass) to settle out while keeping lighter organic solids in suspension.

**Primary treatment** physically removes settleable organic and inorganic suspended solids. Screened sewage enters large **primary sedimentation tanks** (clarifiers) and is retained for a settling period selected for the plant design. Gravity settles heavy suspended particles to the bottom as **primary sludge**, while surface skimmers remove floating oils and grease.

> **Watch out.** Do not conflate preliminary screening with primary sedimentation, and do not assume every plant adds chemical coagulants during primary settling. Standard primary clarification relies on gravity alone; coagulants like alum ($\text{Al}_2(\text{SO}_4)_3$) are added only in *chemically enhanced primary treatment* when colloidal destabilization is specifically required:
$$
\text{Al}_2(\text{SO}_4)_3 + 6\text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow 2\text{Al(OH)}_3\downarrow + 3\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4
$$
Where gelatinous $\text{Al(OH)}_3$ flocs sweep fine suspended impurities out of suspension.

### Secondary (Biological) Treatment and the Activated Sludge Process

Primary effluent still contains high concentrations of dissolved and colloidal biodegradable organic matter. **Secondary treatment** uses microorganisms to metabolize these organics. While aerobic systems (activated sludge, trickling filters) are standard for municipal sewage, anaerobic systems like the Upflow Anaerobic Sludge Blanket (UASB) reactor are also viable secondary options, especially in warm climates.

Under aerobic conditions, heterotrophic bacteria oxidize organic carbon to carbon dioxide and water. Organic nitrogen (such as urea, $\text{NH}_2\text{CONH}_2$) is converted to ammonia ($\text{NH}_3$/$\text{NH}_4^+$); separate nitrifying microorganisms can then oxidize it to nitrite and nitrate under suitable operating conditions:

$$
\text{NH}_2\text{CONH}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \xrightarrow{\text{urease}} \text{CO}_2 + 2\text{NH}_3
$$

$$
2\text{NH}_4^+ + 3\text{O}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{Nitrosomonas}} 2\text{NO}_2^- + 4\text{H}^+ + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

$$
2\text{NO}_2^- + \text{O}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{Nitrobacter}} 2\text{NO}_3^-
$$

#### The Activated Sludge Process
The activated sludge process is a suspended-growth aerobic biological system comprising two linked units: an **aeration tank** and a **secondary clarifier**.

1. **Aeration and bio-flocculation:** Primary effluent enters the aeration tank and mixes with a dense culture of microorganisms (bacteria, protozoa) called **activated sludge** (forming the *mixed liquor*). Mechanical surface aerators or bottom air diffusers continuously supply oxygen and turbulence. Microorganisms consume dissolved biodegradable organics for energy and synthesis, clumping together via extracellular polymers into settleable microbial aggregates called **flocs**.
2. **Secondary clarification:** The mixed liquor flows into a quiescent secondary sedimentation tank. Gravity separates the biological flocs from the treated water, producing a clear supernatant effluent.
3. **Sludge recycling and wasting:** A controlled fraction of the settled biomass—**Return Activated Sludge (RAS)**—is pumped back to the aeration tank inlet to maintain a high microbial population and rapid oxidation rate. Because microbial growth continuously generates excess biomass, the remaining portion—**Waste Activated Sludge (WAS)**—is withdrawn and sent to sludge treatment.

> **Watch out.** Secondary biological treatment converts dissolved *biodegradable organic matter* into $\text{CO}_2$, water, and settleable microbial biomass; it does not remove all dissolved inorganic minerals or heavy metals.

### Tertiary Treatment, Disinfection, and Sludge Management

Secondary effluent may still contain inorganic nutrients (nitrogen and phosphorus) that trigger eutrophication in receiving waters, alongside residual pathogens. **Tertiary (advanced) treatment** polishes the water:

* **Phosphorus removal (chemical precipitation):** Adding lime ($\text{CaO}$ or $\text{Ca(OH)}_2$) at elevated pH precipitates dissolved orthophosphates as calcium phosphate phases. Alum and ferric salts instead form aluminium- or iron-associated phosphate solids and flocs. A simplified calcium phosphate precipitation equation is:
$$
3\text{Ca}^{2+} + 2\text{PO}_4^{3-} \longrightarrow \text{Ca}_3(\text{PO}_4)_2\downarrow
$$
* **Nitrogen removal:** Achieved biologically via sequential **nitrification** (aerobic oxidation of $\text{NH}_4^+$ to $\text{NO}_3^-$) and **denitrification** (anoxic bacterial reduction of $\text{NO}_3^-$ to harmless $\text{N}_2$ gas), or physico-chemically via **ammonia stripping** (raising pH sufficiently to convert $\text{NH}_4^+$ to volatile $\text{NH}_3$ gas, followed by counter-current air contact in packed towers—baffle plates alone cannot strip ammonia without pH adjustment and air flow).
* **Disinfection:** Chlorine ($\text{Cl}_2$ or hypochlorite), ozone, or UV radiation destroys remaining pathogenic bacteria and viruses. When chlorine is used, **dechlorination** (e.g., adding sulfur dioxide or sodium bisulfite) is often required before discharge to protect aquatic organisms from toxic chlorine residuals.

Tertiary treatment does not automatically make water potable or fit for unrestricted discharge; final water quality criteria depend strictly on the receiving body or intended reuse (e.g., irrigation, industrial cooling).

**Sludge handling:** Primary sludge and waste activated sludge contain $95\text{–}99\%$ water and putrescible organics. They undergo **thickening**, **anaerobic digestion** (where anaerobic microorganisms stabilize organics and methanogenic archaea generate methane-rich biogas), and **dewatering** (via centrifuges or belt presses) before managed land application, composting, or landfilling.

### Self-Check Questions

1. **Why must a portion of the settled sludge in the secondary clarifier be continuously recycled to the aeration tank?**
   *Answer:* Recycling Return Activated Sludge (RAS) maintains a high concentration of acclimated, active microorganisms in the aeration tank, allowing incoming dissolved organic matter to be oxidized rapidly within a practical tank volume and retention time.
2. **What is the chemical role of lime ($\text{CaO}$) in tertiary sewage treatment?**
   *Answer:* Lime reacts with water to raise the pH and supply $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ ions, precipitating dissolved phosphate ions ($\text{PO}_4^{3-}$) as insoluble calcium phosphate to prevent eutrophication in receiving waters.

## Trickling filters and the UASB process

Secondary biological sewage treatment relies on microorganisms to degrade dissolved and colloidal organic pollutants remaining after primary sedimentation. While the activated sludge process keeps microbes suspended in aerated liquid, alternative reactors immobilize biomass on solid surfaces or within dense, self-settling granules. Two widely used systems representing aerobic attached-growth and anaerobic granular-sludge technologies are the **trickling filter** and the **Upflow Anaerobic Sludge Blanket (UASB)** reactor.

### Trickling filters (aerobic attached-growth treatment)

Despite its name, a trickling filter does not operate by physical straining or sieving. It is an **aerobic attached-growth biological reactor** in which microorganisms grow as a slimy biofilm (zoogleal film) on a fixed packing medium. Why use a packed bed with large voids? The wide spaces between the packing pieces allow air to circulate freely by natural draft or forced ventilation while wastewater trickles downward as a thin liquid film, ensuring simultaneous access to dissolved organic substrates and atmospheric oxygen.

![Cross-sectional diagram of a trickling filter showing the rotating distributor arm, packing media bed, underdrain system, and effluent outlet.](/notes/cse/s1/chemistry/module-4/assets/page-026-image-1.png)

A typical trickling filter is a circular or rectangular bed (with depth selected for its media and loading) packed with high-surface-area materials such as crushed rocks, broken bricks, slag, or engineered plastic modules. Treatment proceeds through the following steps:

1. **Distribution of pre-treated sewage:** Primary clarified sewage (freed of coarse screenings and settleable grit/solids that would otherwise clog the bed) is sprayed evenly over the top of the packing media through a **rotating distributor arm**.
2. **Biofilm contact and aerobic oxidation:** As the sewage trickles downward over the media surfaces, aerobic bacteria, fungi, and protozoa within the attached biofilm absorb and oxidize dissolved organic compounds into stable end products ($\text{CO}_2$, $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, and oxidized nitrogen species such as $\text{NO}_2^-$ and $\text{NO}_3^-$).

$$
\text{Organic matter} + \text{O}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{Aerobic biofilm}} \text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{New biomass}
$$

3. **Sloughing of excess biomass:** As the microbial layer thickens, oxygen cannot diffuse all the way to the inner face attached to the rock or plastic. The inner microbes enter an endogenous or anaerobic state and lose their grip, causing patches of old biofilm to detach—a natural renewal process called **sloughing**.
4. **Underdrain collection and secondary clarification:** Treated liquid and sloughed biofilm fragments exit through a perforated **underdrain system** at the bottom of the tank, which also serves as an air inlet. The liquid then passes into a **secondary clarifier (humus tank)** to settle out the sloughed biomass. Part of the clarified effluent may be recirculated to the filter inlet to keep the biofilm moist and dilute high-strength inflows.

### Upflow Anaerobic Sludge Blanket (UASB) process

> **Watch out.** Student notes frequently expand UASB as "Upflow Anaerobic Sludge Bed" and claim that it treats the solid screenings removed during preliminary bar screening. Both are incorrect: UASB stands for **Upflow Anaerobic Sludge Blanket**, and it treats liquid wastewater (such as primary clarified sewage or high-strength industrial effluent) rather than coarse solid trash.

The UASB reactor is a high-rate **anaerobic biological treatment** system that operates without any oxygen supply or mechanical aeration. Why feed the wastewater from the bottom? Introducing liquid upward at a controlled hydraulic velocity brings the wastewater into contact with a retained sludge blanket while washing out treated water at the top, achieving intense contact between organic pollutants and microbes without mechanical mixing.

![Schematic diagram of a UASB reactor showing bottom influent feed, anaerobic granular sludge blanket, biogas collector dome, and top effluent outlet.](/notes/cse/s1/chemistry/module-4/assets/page-027-image-1.png)

Operation of a UASB reactor involves four main features:

1. **Bottom feed and upward flow:** Pre-treated liquid sewage enters through distribution nozzles at the bottom of the reactor and flows vertically upward. Designers select the upflow velocity according to reactor design and wastewater strength so that the sludge blanket stays suspended without being washed out.
2. **Granular sludge blanket:** The lower zone contains a dense blanket of biologically formed **sludge granules** ($1\text{–}5\text{ mm}$ in diameter). These granules are compact, self-immobilized consortia of anaerobic microorganisms, including bacteria and methanogenic archaea with high settling velocities.
3. **Anaerobic conversion and biogas generation:** As wastewater passes through the blanket, anaerobic microorganisms degrade organic matter in sequential stages—hydrolysis and **acidogenesis** (acid-forming bacteria converting complex organics into volatile fatty acids, $\text{CO}_2$, and $\text{H}_2$) followed by **methanogenesis** (methane-forming archaea converting intermediate acids and hydrogen into methane).

$$
\text{Organic matter} \xrightarrow{\text{Acid-forming microbes}} \text{Volatile organic acids} + \text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{Methanogens}} \text{CH}_4\uparrow + \text{CO}_2\uparrow
$$

   The overall process is **anaerobic digestion**, involving fermentation and linked oxidation–reduction reactions without free molecular oxygen.
4. **Three-phase separation (Gas–Liquid–Solids Separator, GLSS):** Rising **biogas** bubbles (primarily $\text{CH}_4$ and $\text{CO}_2$, which can be captured as a fuel) create natural hydraulic turbulence that mixes the blanket. At the top of the reactor, an inverted cone or **gas collector dome** separates the gas from the liquid and deflects rising sludge granules back down into the settling zone. Clarified liquid effluent overflows from the top Launder. Because anaerobic effluent still contains residual BOD, dissolved $\text{NH}_3$, and $\text{H}_2\text{S}$, it typically requires aerobic polishing and tertiary disinfection (such as chlorination) as required for its intended reuse or discharge.

### Comparison of trickling filter and UASB processes

| Feature | Trickling filter | UASB reactor |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Microbial environment** | Aerobic (requires atmospheric $\text{O}_2$ via natural draft or ventilation) | Strictly anaerobic (operates in the absence of $\text{O}_2$) |
| **Biomass growth form** | Biofilm attached to stationary rock or plastic media | Dense, self-granulated microbial pellets ($1\text{–}5\text{ mm}$) suspended in upflow |
| **Flow direction** | Downflow (trickling over media from top distributor) | Upflow (bottom inlet to top gas–liquid–solids separator) |
| **Energy and byproducts** | Low-to-moderate energy use; produces $\text{CO}_2$, $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, and humus sludge | Can recover energy through $\text{CH}_4$-rich biogas fuel and very little excess sludge |
| **Organic loading & BOD effect** | Best suited for low-to-medium strength sewage; significantly lowers BOD via aerobic oxidation | Handles medium-to-high organic loading; lowers bulk BOD anaerobically but requires aerobic post-treatment |
| **Key operational limitations** | Risk of media clogging (ponding), odour, and filter flies; larger land footprint | Slow initial start-up (granule formation takes weeks/months); sensitive to cold temperatures and toxic shocks |

### Self-check questions

1. **Why is a secondary clarifier required after a trickling filter if the microorganisms are attached to the packing media?**
   *Answer:* As the biofilm grows thicker, the inner layer loses access to oxygen and detaches from the media (sloughing). A secondary clarifier is required to settle and remove this sloughed biomass from the treated effluent.
2. **What prevents the active bacterial biomass from washing out with the top effluent in a UASB reactor?**
   *Answer:* The bacteria form dense, fast-settling granules ($1\text{–}5\text{ mm}$), and the top three-phase (gas–liquid–solids) separator deflects rising granules into a quiescent settling zone while capturing biogas in the collector dome.

## E-waste and resource recovery

### Defining e-waste
Electronic waste (e-waste) refers to discarded, broken, or obsolete electrical and electronic equipment (EEE) that has reached the end of its useful life, and is discarded as waste rather than retained for continued use. Typical examples include discarded computers, mobile phones, office hardware, televisions, and household appliances such as refrigerators.

It is important to distinguish e-waste from functional second-hand products. If a device still works—or is retained for repair or refilling, such as an inkjet cartridge—and is donated or resold for its original purpose, it remains in active service as a reused product rather than entering the waste stream.

### Material composition and major components
E-waste is chemically complex, consisting of four broad material groups: metals, plastics (such as ABS and PET), glass, and ceramics (including silica and alumina substrates). Because product designs change rapidly, exact compositions vary widely by device type and manufacturing era rather than following a fixed universal ratio. E-waste is both a valuable secondary resource and an environmental hazard.

| Component stream | Valuable materials | Potential hazardous substances (device/era-dependent) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Waste printed circuit boards (WPCBs)** | Precious metals ($\text{Au, Ag}$) and base metals ($\text{Cu, Al, Zn, Sn}$); ceramic and polymer substrates | Lead ($\text{Pb}$) in older solders; brominated flame retardants in plastics |
| **Display units (CRTs, LCDs, LEDs)** | Copper yokes, aluminium frames, glass, plastics ($\text{ABS, PET}$) | Lead in cathode ray tube (CRT) funnel glass; mercury ($\text{Hg}$) in cold-cathode fluorescent lamps (CCFLs) used as backlights in older liquid crystal displays (LCDs) |
| **Batteries** | Cobalt, lithium, nickel, lead, copper | Lead in lead-acid batteries; cadmium ($\text{Cd}$) in $\text{Ni-Cd}$ cells; reactive electrolytes in lithium-ion cells |
| **Capacitors and specialized parts** | Aluminium, tantalum, copper leads | Polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs) in legacy dielectric fluids; beryllium oxide ($\text{BeO}$) ceramics in select high-power RF components |

> **Watch out.** Do not confuse the two meanings of **PCB** in e-waste chemistry: **printed circuit boards** are the laminated fiberglass-resin boards that hold electronic components, whereas **polychlorinated biphenyls** (not "bisphenyls") are toxic chlorinated hydrocarbons found in older transformer and capacitor oils. Also note that **LCD** stands for *liquid crystal display* (not "diode"), and while older CCFL-backlit LCDs contain mercury, light-emitting diode (**LED**) backlights do not use mercury lamps.

### Environmental pathways and occupational exposure risks
When e-waste is dumped in unlined landfills, incinerated, or processed through informal backyard recycling (such as open burning of insulated wires or crude acid stripping), toxic substances escape into the environment:
* **Air:** Open burning and uncontrolled smelting release toxic metal fumes ($\text{Pb, Hg, Cd}$), fine particulates, and persistent organic pollutants from brominated flame retardants.
* **Soil and water:** Rainwater percolating through dumped electronics leaches heavy metals and hexavalent chromium ($\text{Cr(VI)}$) into soil and groundwater.
* **Occupational exposure:** Workers handling e-waste without dust collection or personal protective equipment risk inhaling toxic fumes and ceramic dust or ingesting contaminated particulates.

| Hazardous substance | Primary biological and health impacts |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Lead ($\text{Pb}$)** | Impairs central and peripheral nervous systems, blood synthesis, and kidney function; harms childhood brain development. |
| **Cadmium ($\text{Cd}$)** | Bioaccumulates in the kidneys and liver, and can cause kidney and bone damage. |
| **Mercury ($\text{Hg}$)** | Causes chronic damage to the central nervous system and kidneys, along with respiratory and skin disorders. |
| **Beryllium ($\text{Be}$)** | Carcinogenic; inhaling dust or fumes during mechanical shredding can cause chronic beryllium disease (berylliosis). |

### The 4R management hierarchy and formal resource recovery
Sustainable e-waste management follows a four-tier hierarchy—**Reduce, Reuse, Recycle, and Recover**—supported by formal industrial infrastructure.

1. **Reduce:** Minimize waste generation at the source by avoiding unnecessary gadget upgrades, extending device lifespans, and choosing multipurpose equipment.
2. **Reuse:** Extend the functional life of working electronics through second-hand resale, donation, modular repair, or consumable refilling.
3. **Recycle (collection, dismantling, and pre-processing):**
   * **Formal collection:** Segregating e-waste from municipal solid waste so fragile parts are not crushed.
   * **Manual dismantling and depollution:** Trained technicians safely remove hazardous components first—such as batteries, mercury backlights, leaded CRT glass, and legacy capacitors—before mechanical processing.
   * **Shredding and physical separation:** The depolluted hulk is mechanically crushed and shredded, followed by magnetic, eddy-current, and density separation to sort ferrous metals, non-ferrous metals ($\text{Al, Cu}$), and clean plastics.
4. **Recover (metallurgical refining):**
   * **Resource extraction:** Concentrated metal fractions (especially WPCBs) undergo controlled pyrometallurgical smelting, hydrometallurgical leaching, or electrometallurgical refining in facilities equipped with flue-gas scrubbers and effluent treatment.
   * **Circular economy value:** Recovering $\text{Cu, Au, and Ag}$ from “urban mining” conserves virgin ore, lowers energy demand, and directly supports the UN Sustainable Development Goals—specifically **SDG 12** (Responsible Consumption and Production), **SDG 6** (Clean Water and Sanitation, by preventing leachate), and **SDG 3** (Good Health and Well-Being).

### Self-check questions
1. **Why must dismantling and depollution occur before e-waste is mechanically shredded?**
   *Answer:* Shredding intact e-waste would rupture batteries, mercury lamps, and leaded glass, contaminating the recyclable plastic and metal streams and exposing workers to toxic dust and vapours.
2. **Why is urban mining of waste printed circuit boards environmentally preferable to mining virgin ore?**
   *Answer:* WPCBs contain significantly higher concentrations of copper, gold, and silver than natural ores, so well-managed recovery can reduce virgin material demand and mining impacts while keeping hazardous materials out of uncontrolled disposal. Energy benefits depend on the feed and recovery process.

## Greenhouse gases and climate change

Environmental chemistry distinguishes carefully between atmospheric timescales and radiative processes. **Weather** describes short-term atmospheric conditions (temperature, humidity, wind, and precipitation) over hours or days in a specific region. **Climate** represents the long-term statistical average of weather patterns over decades or centuries. **Global warming** refers specifically to the sustained rise in Earth's average surface temperature, whereas **climate change** encompasses long-term shifts in temperature, precipitation and other climate characteristics; it can have natural or human causes.

### Mechanism of the greenhouse effect

The Earth's surface temperature is governed by a radiative balance between incoming solar energy and outgoing terrestrial heat:

1. **Solar transmission and surface absorption:** Incoming solar radiation consists mainly of visible light and short-wavelength radiation, some of which is reflected or absorbed by the atmosphere and clouds, while the remainder warms the Earth's land and oceans.
2. **Terrestrial longwave emission:** The warmed planetary surface radiates energy back toward space as longer-wavelength thermal infrared (IR) radiation.
3. **Molecular absorption and re-emission:** Greenhouse gases (GHGs) in the lower atmosphere absorb specific wavelengths of this outgoing terrestrial IR radiation and re-emit it in all directions, returning a portion of the thermal energy to the surface and lower troposphere.

> **Watch out.** Greenhouse gases do not primarily warm the planet by directly trapping incoming ultraviolet (UV) or shortwave solar rays on their way in. Instead, they absorb outgoing longwave thermal infrared radiation emitted by the warmed Earth.

#### Why only certain gases absorb infrared radiation

To absorb infrared radiation, a molecule must possess **infrared-active vibrational modes**—meaning its dipole moment must change as its bonds stretch or bend.

* **Inactive bulk gases:** Homonuclear diatomic molecules such as $\text{N}_2$ and $\text{O}_2$ (which make up the bulk of dry air) have symmetric charge distributions; stretching their single bond creates no dipole change, so they are transparent to thermal IR.
* **Active greenhouse gases:** Polyatomic molecules such as carbon dioxide ($\text{CO}_2$, via asymmetric stretching and bending modes), water vapour ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$, a bent polar molecule), methane ($\text{CH}_4$), nitrous oxide ($\text{N}_2\text{O}$), tropospheric ozone ($\text{O}_3$), and synthetic fluorinated gases (such as chlorofluorocarbons, CFCs, and hydrofluorocarbons) undergo significant dipole changes during vibration and strongly absorb outgoing IR.

### Natural versus enhanced greenhouse effect

The **natural greenhouse effect** is essential for life; without background levels of water vapour and $\text{CO}_2$, Earth's average surface temperature would be well below the freezing point of water. However, human activities since the industrial era have increased atmospheric concentrations of long-lived greenhouse gases, driving an **enhanced (anthropogenic) greenhouse effect** that traps additional heat.

Water vapour is the most abundant greenhouse gas, but its atmospheric lifetime is only days and its concentration is controlled directly by air temperature (evaporation and condensation). Consequently, water vapour acts mainly as an **amplifying feedback**—warmer air holds more moisture, which traps more heat—rather than a primary anthropogenic forcing agent.

#### Anthropogenic versus natural sources

Human-driven emissions originate from four primary sectors:

* **Fossil fuel combustion:** Burning coal, oil, and natural gas for electricity, heating, and transport releases fossil $\text{CO}_2$:
$$
\text{CH}_4 + 2\text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{CO}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}
$$
* **Deforestation and land-use change:** Clearing forests removes photosynthetic carbon sinks and oxidizes biomass carbon into $\text{CO}_2$.
* **Agriculture:** Flooded rice paddies and ruminant livestock digestion generate $\text{CH}_4$ via anaerobic fermentation, while nitrogen fertilizers undergo microbial nitrification and denitrification to release $\text{N}_2\text{O}$.
* **Industrial processes:** Cement calcination ($\text{CaCO}_3 \rightarrow \text{CaO} + \text{CO}_2$) releases $\text{CO}_2$, and refrigeration, foam blowing, and electronics manufacturing release synthetic fluorinated gases.

> **Watch out.** Volcanic eruptions are a *natural* geological source of $\text{CO}_2$ and aerosols, not an anthropogenic (human-caused) driver of climate change.

### Consequences of climate change

* **Thermal extremes and altered hydrological cycles:** Higher temperatures accelerate evaporation in arid zones (intensifying droughts and crop stress) while increasing the moisture capacity of air, leading to intense rainfall and flooding elsewhere.
* **Sea-level rise:** Global sea level rises through two primary physical mechanisms: the **thermal expansion** of ocean water as it warms, and the **melting of land-based ice** (mountain glaciers and the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets). Note that the melting of floating sea ice does not directly raise sea level because floating ice already displaces its own mass of water, though its loss reduces surface reflectivity (albedo).
* **Ecological and health impacts:** Warming waters experience reduced dissolved oxygen and thermal stress that harm aquatic ecosystems, while shifts in temperature and rainfall can change the distribution of disease vectors and exposure risks.

*(Note: While CFCs act as greenhouse gases in the troposphere, stratospheric ozone depletion itself is a distinct photochemical issue concerning UV radiation rather than the principal driver of global warming; this is examined in the next section.)*

### Mitigation versus adaptation

Engineering responses to climate change are divided into reducing the cause (**mitigation**) and managing the unavoidable impacts (**adaptation**).

| Feature | Climate Mitigation | Climate Adaptation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Core objective** | Reduce net greenhouse gas emissions or enhance carbon sinks to limit the magnitude of future warming. | Adjust infrastructure, agriculture, and communities to withstand current and projected climate impacts. |
| **Timescale and spatial scope** | Long-term, global benefit (since well-mixed GHGs affect the entire planet). | Immediate to medium-term, localized to regional vulnerability. |
| **Engineering examples** | Transitioning to solar, wind, and nuclear power; carbon capture and storage (CCS); electrifying transport; industrial energy efficiency; afforestation. | Constructing seawalls and storm-surge barriers; upgrading urban stormwater drainage; breeding drought-tolerant crops; heat-resilient building design. |

### Self-check questions

1. **Question:** Why is $\text{CO}_2$ an active greenhouse gas whereas $\text{N}_2$, which makes up most of the atmosphere, is not?
   **Answer:** $\text{CO}_2$ has asymmetric stretching and bending vibrations that alter its molecular dipole moment, allowing it to absorb outgoing thermal infrared radiation. $\text{N}_2$ is a symmetric homonuclear diatomic molecule whose vibration produces no dipole change, making it transparent to thermal IR.

2. **Question:** Why is atmospheric water vapour classified as a climate feedback rather than a primary anthropogenic forcing agent?
   **Answer:** Water vapour concentration is governed thermodynamically by atmospheric temperature via rapid evaporation and precipitation rather than direct human emissions; warming caused by long-lived gases like $\text{CO}_2$ increases evaporation, which then amplifies the warming.

## Stratospheric ozone and ozone depletion

Ozone ($\text{O}_3$) plays two opposing environmental roles depending on its altitude. In the lower atmosphere (troposphere), ground-level ozone is a harmful secondary pollutant and respiratory irritant formed by photochemical smog reactions. In the stratosphere, where most ozone lies roughly $15\text{--}35\text{ km}$ above Earth's surface, the ozone layer acts as a vital protective radiation shield. It absorbs biologically damaging solar ultraviolet (specifically UV-B and UV-C) radiation before it can reach the biosphere.

### Natural formation and the Chapman cycle

The Chapman cycle describes basic photochemical ozone formation and destruction. Actual stratospheric ozone also depends on transport and catalytic reactions:

1. **Photodissociation of oxygen:** High-energy short-wave UV photons ($h\nu$, where $h$ is Planck's constant and $\nu$ is frequency) cleave molecular oxygen into reactive oxygen atoms (radicals):
$$
\text{O}_2(g) + h\nu \longrightarrow 2\text{O}^\bullet(g)
$$

2. **Ozone formation:** An oxygen radical combines with molecular oxygen in the presence of a neutral third body $\text{M}$ (typically $\text{N}_2$ or $\text{O}_2$). The collision partner $\text{M}$ is not consumed; it absorbs excess vibrational energy so the newly formed $\text{O}_3$ bond does not immediately rupture:
$$
\text{O}^\bullet(g) + \text{O}_2(g) + \text{M}(g) \longrightarrow \text{O}_3(g) + \text{M}(g)
$$

3. **Photodissociation of ozone:** Ozone absorbs solar UV-B radiation, shielding the Earth and regenerating $\text{O}_2$ and $\text{O}^\bullet$ (which quickly reform $\text{O}_3$ via step 2, converting UV energy into heat):
$$
\text{O}_3(g) + h\nu \longrightarrow \text{O}_2(g) + \text{O}^\bullet(g)
$$

4. **Natural recombination:** When an oxygen radical collides with an ozone molecule, both odd-oxygen species are destroyed:
$$
\text{O}^\bullet(g) + \text{O}_3(g) \longrightarrow 2\text{O}_2(g)
$$

> **Watch out.** Do not write ozone formation as $\text{O}^\bullet + \text{O}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{UV}} \text{O}_3$. The combination of $\text{O}^\bullet$ and $\text{O}_2$ is exothermic and requires a collision partner $\text{M}$ to carry away energy, whereas UV light actually drives the reverse photodissociation of $\text{O}_3$.

### Catalytic ozone depletion

Ozone depletion is the anthropogenic thinning of the stratospheric ozone layer because chemical catalysts accelerate step 4 above its natural rate. The primary culprits are chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs, or Freons, such as $\text{CF}_2\text{Cl}_2$), historically used as refrigerants, aerosol propellants, and foam-blowing agents.

Because CFCs are chemically inert and water-insoluble in the troposphere, they slowly mix upward into the stratosphere. There, intense UV radiation cleaves the carbon-chlorine bond by photolysis to release free chlorine radicals ($\text{Cl}^\bullet$):
$$
\text{CF}_2\text{Cl}_2(g) + h\nu \longrightarrow \text{CF}_2\text{Cl}^\bullet(g) + \text{Cl}^\bullet(g)
$$

The chlorine radical acts as a homogeneous catalyst in a chain reaction:
$$
\text{Cl}^\bullet(g) + \text{O}_3(g) \longrightarrow \text{ClO}^\bullet(g) + \text{O}_2(g)
$$
$$
\text{ClO}^\bullet(g) + \text{O}^\bullet(g) \longrightarrow \text{Cl}^\bullet(g) + \text{O}_2(g)
$$

**Net reaction:**
$$
\text{O}_3(g) + \text{O}^\bullet(g) \longrightarrow 2\text{O}_2(g)
$$

Because $\text{Cl}^\bullet$ is regenerated at the end of each cycle, a single chlorine radical can destroy thousands of ozone molecules before being removed into inactive reservoir species. In the frigid polar spring, reactions on polar stratospheric clouds activate chlorine. After sunlight returns, gas-phase cycles such as the chlorine monoxide dimer cycle destroy ozone without requiring free $\text{O}^\bullet$ atoms.

> **Watch out.** In the second step of the standard gas-phase chlorine cycle, $\text{ClO}^\bullet$ reacts with an oxygen radical ($\text{O}^\bullet$), not with another $\text{O}_3$ molecule. Writing $\text{ClO}^\bullet + \text{O}_3 \longrightarrow 2\text{O}_2 + \text{Cl}^\bullet$ is a common transcription error.

#### Depletion by nitrogen oxides

Nitric oxide ($\text{NO}^\bullet$) emitted directly into the stratosphere by high-altitude supersonic aircraft or formed from nitrous oxide ($\text{N}_2\text{O}$) also catalyses ozone destruction via an analogous radical cycle:
$$
\text{NO}^\bullet(g) + \text{O}_3(g) \longrightarrow \text{NO}_2^\bullet(g) + \text{O}_2(g)
$$
$$
\text{NO}_2^\bullet(g) + \text{O}^\bullet(g) \longrightarrow \text{NO}^\bullet(g) + \text{O}_2(g)
$$

### Impacts and mitigation

When stratospheric ozone thins, higher fluxes of high-energy UV-B radiation penetrate to the Earth's surface:
* **Human health:** Causes DNA pyrimidine dimerization, increasing skin cancers (melanoma and non-melanoma), sunburns, and ocular cataracts.
* **Ecosystems and crops:** Damages marine phytoplankton at the base of aquatic food webs and inhibits photosynthesis and leaf expansion in terrestrial plants.

To halt this damage, the international community adopted the **Montreal Protocol** (1987) to phase out the production and consumption of ozone-depleting substances like CFCs, replacing them with less destructive alternatives and promoting recovery of the stratospheric shield.

### Distinguishing ozone depletion from the greenhouse effect

Students frequently confuse stratospheric ozone depletion with global climate change. Although CFCs happen to be both ozone-depleting substances and greenhouse gases, the two physical mechanisms are distinct:

| Feature | Stratospheric Ozone Depletion | Enhanced Greenhouse Effect |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Primary radiation involved** | Incoming solar short-wave ultraviolet (UV-B) | Outgoing terrestrial long-wave infrared (IR) |
| **Physical/chemical mechanism** | Catalytic free-radical destruction of stratospheric $\text{O}_3$ | Absorption and emission of terrestrial infrared radiation by greenhouse gases |
| **Main anthropogenic drivers** | CFCs, halons, stratospheric $\text{NO}_x$ | $\text{CO}_2$, $\text{CH}_4$, $\text{N}_2\text{O}$, fluorinated gases |
| **Primary consequence** | Increased surface UV flux (skin cancer, cataracts, crop/plankton damage) | Tropospheric warming, sea-level rise, and altered climate patterns |

### Self-check questions

1. **Why can a small concentration of stratospheric $\text{CF}_2\text{Cl}_2$ destroy a vastly larger molar quantity of $\text{O}_3$?**
   *Answer:* Photolysis of $\text{CF}_2\text{Cl}_2$ releases a $\text{Cl}^\bullet$ radical that oxidises $\text{O}_3$ to form $\text{ClO}^\bullet$, which then reacts with $\text{O}^\bullet$ to regenerate $\text{Cl}^\bullet$. Because the radical catalyst is continuously recycled, one $\text{Cl}^\bullet$ atom repeats the destruction cycle thousands of times.

2. **What is the role of $\text{M}$ in the reaction $\text{O}^\bullet + \text{O}_2 + \text{M} \longrightarrow \text{O}_3 + \text{M}$?**
   *Answer:* $\text{M}$ is an inert third-body collision partner (such as $\text{N}_2$ or $\text{O}_2$) that removes excess kinetic energy from the newly formed $\text{O}_3$ molecule, preventing it from immediately dissociating back into $\text{O}^\bullet$ and $\text{O}_2$.

## Sustainable development and the 17 SDGs

### Concept of sustainable development

Sustainable development, as defined by the Brundtland Commission, is development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. It is built on the principle of **intergenerational fairness**: current resource consumption, industrial growth, and waste generation must not degrade the ecological systems that future populations will depend upon.

Achieving sustainability requires integrating environmental protection, social equity, and economic viability into long-term planning. Why is this integration necessary? An industrial process that maximizes short-term profit by discharging untreated effluent may look economically successful today, but it shifts heavy remediation and healthcare costs onto society and future generations.

> **Watch out.** Sustainable development does not mean infinite, unconstrained economic growth, nor does it mean halting all industrial activity. Because Earth has finite resources and limited capacity to assimilate waste, sustainable engineering focuses on long-term resource efficiency, circular material flows, and staying within ecological boundaries.

### The three pillars of sustainability

Sustainable decision-making balances three interconnected pillars. A project is truly sustainable only where all three overlap.

![Venn diagram of the three pillars of sustainable development: Social, Environmental, and Economic, with their intersection labeled 'sustainable'.](/notes/cse/s1/chemistry/module-4/assets/page-051-image-1.png)
*Figure: The three overlapping pillars of sustainable development—Environmental, Social, and Economic—intersecting to define sustainable practice.*

| Pillar | Core objective | Engineering application (e.g., water & materials) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Environmental protection** | Preserve ecosystems, biodiversity, and natural resources through pollution control and waste minimization. | Treating sewage to lower BOD before discharge; recycling e-waste metals; water reuse and zero-liquid-discharge systems. |
| **Social inclusion** | Ensure equitable access to resources, health, safety, and opportunities across all demographics and generations. | Providing safe, pathogen-free drinking water to all communities; preventing toxic e-waste exposure among informal workers. |
| **Economic prosperity** | Maintain efficient, productive systems that deliver long-term value without externalizing environmental or social harm. | Designing treatment plants for lifecycle affordability, energy recovery (such as UASB biogas), and manageable maintenance costs. |

### The 17 Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)

In 2015, the United Nations adopted the **2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development**, establishing 17 interconnected global goals to address poverty, inequality, environmental degradation, and peace:

1. **No Poverty**
2. **Zero Hunger**
3. **Good Health and Well-being**
4. **Quality Education**
5. **Gender Equality**
6. **Clean Water and Sanitation**
7. **Affordable and Clean Energy**
8. **Decent Work and Economic Growth**
9. **Industry, Innovation and Infrastructure**
10. **Reduced Inequalities**
11. **Sustainable Cities and Communities**
12. **Responsible Consumption and Production**
13. **Climate Action**
14. **Life Below Water**
15. **Life on Land**
16. **Peace, Justice and Strong Institutions**
17. **Partnerships for the Goals**

### Connecting environmental chemistry to the SDGs

The chemical and treatment processes studied across this module directly support several SDGs:

* **SDG 6 (Clean Water and Sanitation):** Supported by water purification—coagulation, filtration, and disinfection (chlorination, ozonation, UV)—alongside biological sewage treatment (activated sludge, trickling filters, UASB) and desalination. Note that water softening (ion exchange) primarily protects industrial boilers and piping from scale; safe drinking water requires pathogen disinfection and toxic contaminant removal, not softening alone.
* **SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-being):** Disinfecting drinking water prevents waterborne diseases, while proper e-waste handling prevents heavy-metal poisoning from lead, mercury, and cadmium.
* **SDG 12 (Responsible Consumption and Production):** Hydrometallurgical and pyrometallurgical recovery of precious and base metals from e-waste reduces virgin ore mining and hazardous landfilling.
* **SDG 13 (Climate Action):** Mitigating greenhouse gas emissions ($\text{CO}_2$, $\text{CH}_4$, $\text{N}_2\text{O}$, CFCs) and capturing methane from anaerobic digesters (UASB) for fuel directly limit global warming.
* **SDG 7 (Affordable and Clean Energy):** Transitioning from fossil fuels to renewable energy and recovering biogas from wastewater sludge improve energy sustainability.
* **SDG 14 (Life Below Water) and SDG 15 (Life on Land):** Removing organic load (BOD/COD) and nutrients (nitrates and phosphates) during tertiary sewage treatment prevents eutrophication and hypoxia in aquatic ecosystems, while controlling acid rain and hazardous waste protects terrestrial habitats.

### Engineering trade-offs in sustainable design

No engineering technology offers an unlimited environmental benefit; every solution involves measurable trade-offs across the three pillars.

> **Watch out.** Do not treat pollution-control or water-treatment technologies as having zero environmental cost. For example, **reverse osmosis (RO)** produces low-salinity permeate from brackish or saline sources; drinking-water use also requires appropriate post-treatment (supporting SDG 6), but high-pressure pumps consume significant electrical energy (challenging SDG 7 and SDG 13) and generate concentrated **reject brine** that can harm marine life if discharged improperly (challenging SDG 14).

Similarly, breakpoint chlorination provides reliable residual disinfection, yet reaction with natural organic matter can form carcinogenic trihalomethanes (THMs). Ozonation and UV avoid chlorinated by-products but require higher capital and electrical input and leave no protective residual in distribution pipes. Sustainable engineering therefore requires **lifecycle thinking**—quantifying energy demand, chemical consumption, by-product disposal, and social affordability before selecting a process.

### Self-check questions

1. **Question:** How does the Brundtland definition of sustainable development connect current engineering choices to future generations?
   **Answer:** It requires that present needs be met without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs, meaning engineers must prevent irreversible resource depletion and long-term environmental pollution.

2. **Question:** Give one example of an engineering trade-off between SDG 6 (Clean Water and Sanitation) and SDG 13 (Climate Action) or SDG 14 (Life Below Water) in seawater desalination.
   **Answer:** Reverse osmosis desalination supplies clean water (SDG 6), but requires high electrical energy—often increasing greenhouse gas emissions (SDG 13)—and produces concentrated brine that can damage ecosystems without suitable concentrate management (SDG 14).

## Revision sheet and practice problems

### Core Formulas and Concept Sheet

#### Water Hardness and $\text{CaCO}_3$ Equivalents
Calcium carbonate ($\text{CaCO}_3$, molar mass $= 100\text{ g/mol}$, equivalent mass $= 50\text{ g/eq}$) is the universal reference standard because it is a familiar sparingly soluble scale-forming compound and its rounded mass values simplify stoichiometry. To convert any hardness-producing species into $\text{mg/L}$ (or $\text{ppm}$) as $\text{CaCO}_3$ equivalent:

$$
H = \frac{\text{Mass of species (mg/L)} \times 50}{\text{Equivalent mass of species}}
$$

For solutions of known normality $N$ (where $N = \text{Molarity} \times \text{total positive charge of multivalent hardness cations}$):

$$
H\text{ (mg/L as CaCO}_3\text{)} = N \times 50 \times 1000 = N \times 50000
$$

| Species | Mass ($\text{g/mol}$) | Eq. Mass ($\text{g/eq}$) | Species | Mass ($\text{g/mol}$) | Eq. Mass ($\text{g/eq}$) |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :--- | :---: | :---: |
| $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ | $40$ | $20$ | $\text{Ca(HCO}_3\text{)}_2$ | $162$ | $81$ |
| $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ | $24$ | $12$ | $\text{Mg(HCO}_3\text{)}_2$ | $146$ | $73$ |
| $\text{HCO}_3^-$ | $61$ | $61$ | $\text{CaSO}_4$ | $136$ | $68$ |
| $\text{CaCl}_2$ | $111$ | $55.5$ | $\text{MgSO}_4$ | $120$ | $60$ |
| $\text{MgCl}_2$ | $95$ | $47.5$ | $\text{NaHCO}_3$ | $84$ | $84$ |

#### Partitioning Hardness (Ion-Balance Rules)
* **Total Hardness ($\text{TH}$):** Sum of $\text{CaCO}_3$ equivalents of calcium and magnesium in routine natural-water hardness analysis ($\text{Ca}^{2+}$ and $\text{Mg}^{2+}$). Monovalent ions ($\text{Na}^+, \text{K}^+$) do not cause hardness.
* **Carbonate / Temporary Hardness ($\text{CH}$):** Bounded by both total hardness and total bicarbonate/carbonate alkalinity ($\text{Alk}$):

$$
\text{CH} = \min(\text{TH}, \text{Alk})
$$

* **Non-Carbonate / Permanent Hardness ($\text{NCH}$):** The remaining hardness that cannot be removed by boiling:

$$
\text{NCH} = \text{TH} - \text{CH} \ge 0
$$

> **Watch out.** Adding $\text{NaCl}$ does not change total, temporary, or permanent hardness because $\text{Na}^+$ cannot precipitate soap. Adding $\text{NaHCO}_3$ increases alkalinity, which can convert permanent hardness into temporary hardness, but **Carbonate Hardness can never exceed Total Hardness** ($\text{NCH}$ is never negative).

#### Unit Conversions and Oxygen Demand
* **Hardness Units:** $1\text{ mg/L as CaCO}_3 \approx 1\text{ ppm}$ for dilute water; $1^\circ\text{French} = 10\text{ mg/L as CaCO}_3$; $1^\circ\text{Clarke} \approx 14.25\text{ mg/L as CaCO}_3$ (one grain per Imperial gallon).
* **Unseeded $\text{BOD}_5$:** For initial dissolved oxygen $\text{DO}_i$, 5-day dissolved oxygen $\text{DO}_f$, and dilution factor $P = \frac{V_{\text{sample}}}{V_{\text{total}}}$:

$$
\text{BOD}_5\text{ (mg/L)} = \frac{\text{DO}_i - \text{DO}_f}{P}
$$

### Quick Retrieval Questions

1. **What happens when bicarbonate hardness is heated?**
   *Answer:* Calcium bicarbonate can precipitate as calcium carbonate. Prolonged heating of magnesium bicarbonate can ultimately form magnesium hydroxide. Removal depends on conditions and is not perfectly quantitative.
2. **How are the two demineralization beds regenerated?**
   *Answer:* The hydrogen-form cation resin ($RH$) is regenerated with acid; the hydroxide-form anion resin ($R'OH$) with sodium hydroxide. Sodium-cycle softening is different: it removes calcium and magnesium but retains dissolved sodium salts.
3. **What drives reverse osmosis?**
   *Answer:* The applied transmembrane pressure difference must exceed the osmotic pressure difference, with sufficient additional driving force for useful water flow.
4. **What does breakpoint chlorination signify?**
   *Answer:* Much of the ammonia-related demand has been satisfied and combined chlorine destroyed; further dosing produces a rising free-chlorine residual. It does not imply complete oxidation of all organic matter.
5. **Why use dechlorination?**
   *Answer:* Where chlorine removal is required, reducing agents such as sulphite or treatment with activated carbon remove residual chlorine. Distribution systems may still require a controlled disinfectant residual.
6. **Why is COD usually higher than BOD, and can one be calculated from the other?**
   *Answer:* Defined chemical oxidation generally measures a broader oxidizable load than the five-day biological test. This is not a universal inequality, and no universal COD-to-BOD conversion exists. Without a nitrification inhibitor, BOD can include nitrogenous oxygen demand.
7. **How do sewage-treatment stages differ?**
   *Answer:* Preliminary screens and grit chambers protect equipment. Primary settling removes settleable solids. Secondary biological treatment converts biodegradable material and separates biomass. Advanced treatment targets remaining nutrients, solids or pathogens; the actual train depends on the required effluent quality.
8. **How does UASB differ from activated sludge?**
   *Answer:* UASB means Upflow Anaerobic Sludge Blanket. It retains anaerobic microorganisms, including methanogenic archaea, and produces methane-rich biogas. Activated sludge uses suspended flocs, aeration, a clarifier and return sludge.
9. **Why must e-waste be managed separately?**
   *Answer:* It combines valuable metals with potentially hazardous components, depending on device type and age. Collection, depollution, separation and controlled recovery protect workers and avoid environmental releases.
10. **How do greenhouse warming and ozone depletion differ?**
    *Answer:* Greenhouse gases absorb and emit terrestrial infrared radiation. Stratospheric ozone absorbs solar UV; CFC-derived chlorine radicals catalyse its destruction. The two mechanisms involve different radiation and chemistry.
11. **What connects this module to sustainable development?**
    *Answer:* Water treatment supports SDG 6; resource recovery supports SDG 12; emission reduction supports SDG 13. Choices must also account for energy use, waste streams, cost and equitable access.

### Numerical Practice Problems

1. **Single-salt hardness:** Calculate the hardness in $\text{mg/L}$ as $\text{CaCO}_3$ of a water sample containing $136\text{ mg/L}$ of $\text{CaSO}_4$.
   *Solution:* The molar mass of $\text{CaSO}_4 = 40 + 32 + 64 = 136\text{ g/mol}$, and its equivalent mass is $\frac{136}{2} = 68\text{ g/eq}$.
   $$
   H = \frac{136 \times 50}{68} = 100\text{ mg/L as CaCO}_3
   $$

2. **Ion-balance hardness:** A water sample contains $\text{Ca}^{2+} = 40\text{ mg/L}$, $\text{Mg}^{2+} = 12\text{ mg/L}$, and $\text{HCO}_3^- = 122\text{ mg/L}$. Calculate total, carbonate (temporary), and non-carbonate (permanent) hardness.
   *Solution:* Convert each ion to $\text{CaCO}_3$ equivalents using equivalent masses ($\text{Ca}^{2+} = 20$, $\text{Mg}^{2+} = 12$, $\text{HCO}_3^- = 61$):
   $$
   H_{\text{Ca}^{2+}} = \frac{40 \times 50}{20} = 100\text{ mg/L}, \quad H_{\text{Mg}^{2+}} = \frac{12 \times 50}{12} = 50\text{ mg/L}
   $$
   $$
   \text{Alk}_{\text{HCO}_3^-} = \frac{122 \times 50}{61} = 100\text{ mg/L as CaCO}_3
   $$
   $$
   \text{TH} = 100 + 50 = 150\text{ mg/L}, \quad \text{CH} = \min(150, 100) = 100\text{ mg/L}, \quad \text{NCH} = 150 - 100 = 50\text{ mg/L}
   $$

3. **Alkalinity cap and non-hardness salts:** A water sample has a total hardness of $120\text{ mg/L}$ as $\text{CaCO}_3$ and total bicarbonate alkalinity of $160\text{ mg/L}$ as $\text{CaCO}_3$ (due to dissolved $\text{NaHCO}_3$), along with $50\text{ mg/L}$ of $\text{NaCl}$. Determine $\text{TH}$, $\text{CH}$, and $\text{NCH}$.
   *Solution:* $\text{NaCl}$ contributes zero hardness. Because carbonate hardness cannot exceed total hardness:
   $$
   \text{TH} = 120\text{ mg/L}, \quad \text{CH} = \min(\text{TH}, \text{Alk}) = \min(120, 160) = 120\text{ mg/L}, \quad \text{NCH} = 120 - 120 = 0\text{ mg/L}
   $$

4. **Molarity to hardness:** Calculate the hardness of a $0.01\text{ M CaCl}_2$ solution in $\text{mg/L}$ as $\text{CaCO}_3$.
   *Solution:* Each $\text{CaCl}_2$ unit releases one $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ ion (charge $= 2$), so Normality $N = 0.01 \times 2 = 0.02\text{ N}$.
   $$
   H = N \times 50000 = 0.02 \times 50000 = 1000\text{ mg/L as CaCO}_3
   $$

5. **Five-day BOD calculation:** An unseeded wastewater sample diluted to $P = 0.05$ ($5\%$ dilution) has an initial dissolved oxygen of $9.0\text{ mg/L}$, which drops to $4.5\text{ mg/L}$ after $5\text{ days}$ at $20^\circ\text{C}$. Calculate $\text{BOD}_5$.
   *Solution:* Substitute $\text{DO}_i = 9.0\text{ mg/L}$, $\text{DO}_f = 4.5\text{ mg/L}$, and $P = 0.05$:
   $$
   \text{BOD}_5 = \frac{9.0 - 4.5}{0.05} = \frac{4.5}{0.05} = 90\text{ mg/L}
   $$
